\title{The Jacobian Conjecture in Dimension Two:\\
A Research Program via Monomial Algebras,\\
Demazure Roots, and the Inverse Limit}
\author{Research Notes}
\date{October 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We outline a research program toward proving the Jacobian Conjecture in dimension two,
the last remaining open case after Alp\"oge's July 2026 counterexample in dimension three.
Our approach leverages the structural theory of automorphism groups of zero-dimensional
monomial algebras $A_d = \kk[x,y]/\pp^d$ developed by D\'iaz--Dubouloz--Petitjean and
D\'iaz--Lucchini Arteche--Manzano-Flores, particularly the characterization of the
constant-Jacobian subgroup $\GJac(A_d)$ via Demazure roots and the new proof of Anick's
approximation theorem. We propose that the inverse limit structure
$\kk[x,y] \hookrightarrow \varprojlim A_d = \kk[[x,y]]$, combined with the
Jung--van der Kulk theorem and Rentschler's classification of locally nilpotent
derivations in dimension two, may provide the missing bridge from $\pp$-adic approximation
to polynomial invertibility.
\end{abstract}

\section{The Current Landscape (Post-2026)}
%% ====================================================================

\subsection{The Jacobian Conjecture}

\begin{conjecture}[Keller, 1939]
Let $\kk$ be a field of characteristic zero and $F = (F_1,\ldots,F_n): \kk^n \to \kk^n$
a polynomial map with $\det J(F) \in \kk^{\times}$ (a nonzero constant). Then $F$ is a
polynomial automorphism, i.e., $F$ has a polynomial inverse.
\end{conjecture}

\subsection{The 2026 Breakthrough: Dimension $\geq 3$ is False}

In July 2026, L.~Alp\"oge announced an explicit counterexample in dimension three:
a polynomial map $F: \CC^3 \to \CC^3$ with $\det J(F) \equiv -2$ that is not injective
(the generic fiber has 3 points). The construction uses a \emph{tangent sweep} mechanism:
one sweeps the tangent lines of a plane curve---a map that projective duality forces to be
many-to-one---then conjugates by monomial maps to cancel the Jacobian factor.

Gallagher \cite{Gallagher2026} subsequently gave a uniform one-variable construction
producing counterexamples of every geometric degree $d \geq 3$ in every dimension $n \geq 3$.

\begin{theorem}[Alp\"oge 2026, Gallagher 2026]
The Jacobian Conjecture is false in every dimension $n \geq 3$.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Why Dimension Two Survives}

The tangent sweep mechanism is intrinsically three-dimensional: it requires an ``extra
dimension'' to sweep tangent lines of a \emph{plane} curve. In dimension two, several
structural rigidity results prevent the same construction:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \textbf{Jung--van der Kulk theorem:} Every polynomial automorphism of $\kk[x,y]$ is
tame, i.e., a composition of affine and triangular (elementary) automorphisms. The
automorphism group $\Aut_\kk(\kk[x,y])$ is the amalgamated free product of the affine and
triangular subgroups. In dimension $\geq 3$, wild automorphisms exist
(Shestakov--Umirbaev).

\item \textbf{Abhyankar--Moh--Suzuki theorem:} Every embedding $\CC \hookrightarrow \CC^2$
is rectifiable, i.e., equivalent to the standard embedding $t \mapsto (t,0)$ under a
polynomial automorphism. This forces strong constraints on the geometry at infinity of
Keller maps.

\item \textbf{Rentschler's theorem:} Every locally nilpotent derivation of $\kk[x,y]$ is
conjugate to $u(x)\partial_y$ for some $u \in \kk[x]$. This completely classifies
$\Ga$-actions on $\CC^2$.

\item \textbf{Properness obstruction:} A Keller map is \'etale, so failure of bijectivity
requires failure of properness. In dimension two, the behavior at infinity is controlled
by finitely many ``dicritical'' divisors in the resolution of the rational map
$\mathbb{P}^2 \dashrightarrow \mathbb{P}^2$, and the constant Jacobian condition
severely constrains these.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Known Partial Results in Dimension Two}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Moh (1983):} The conjecture holds for $\max(\deg P, \deg Q) \leq 100$.
\item \textbf{Nguyen (2019):} Extended to degree $\leq 104$ using Newton polygon techniques.
\item \textbf{Newton polygon constraints:} For a hypothetical counterexample $(P,Q)$,
  $\gcd(\deg P, \deg Q) \geq 16$ and $\gcd(\deg P, \deg Q) \neq 2p$ for any prime $p$.
\item \textbf{Lee--Li (2022--2024):} Series of papers using Magnus' formula to study
  inner polynomials and the location of vertices of Newton polygons.
\item \textbf{Anick's theorem:} For any $F$ with $\det J(F) \in \kk^\times$ and any $d$,
  there exists a tame automorphism $\varphi_d$ with $F \equiv \varphi_d \pmod{\pp^d}$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Tools from Monomial Algebra Automorphisms}
%% ====================================================================

We now describe the key results from D\'iaz et al.\ that form the foundation of our
approach.

\subsection{Setting}

Let $\kk$ be a field of characteristic zero, $n \geq 1$, and $I \subset \kk[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$
a monomial ideal with $\kk[x]/I$ finite-dimensional (zero-dimensional). The main family of
interest is $A_d = \kk[x_1,\ldots,x_n]/\pp^d$ where $\pp = (x_1,\ldots,x_n)$.

\subsection{Demazure Roots}

\begin{definition}
A \emph{Demazure root} of $I$ is a vector $\alpha \in \ZZ^n$ such that there exists a
homogeneous locally nilpotent derivation $\partial_{\alpha,p}$ of $\kk[x]/I$ of the form
\[
  \partial_{\alpha,p}(x_j) = p_j \cdot x^{\alpha + e_j}
\]
where $p = (p_1,\ldots,p_n) \in \kk^n$ satisfies certain compatibility conditions with $I$.
\end{definition}

The set of Demazure roots $R(I)$ decomposes as:
\[
  R(I) = R_{\mathrm{inn}}(I) \sqcup R_{\mathrm{out}}(I)
\]
where:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Inner roots} $\alpha \in R_{\mathrm{inn}}(I)$: all coordinates of $\alpha + e_j$
  are $\geq 0$, and $x^{\alpha+e_j} \in \kk[x]/I \setminus \{0\}$ for exactly one $j$.
\item \textbf{Outer roots} $\alpha \in R_{\mathrm{out}}(I)$: $\alpha = -e_j + \beta$ where
  $\beta \in \NN^n$ with $\beta_j = 0$, and $\partial_{\alpha,e_j^*}(x_j) = x^\beta$,
  $\partial_{\alpha,e_j^*}(x_i) = 0$ for $i \neq j$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Root Subgroups and the Automorphism Group}

Each root $\alpha$ gives a one-parameter (root) subgroup:
\[
  U_{\alpha,p} = \{\exp(t \cdot \partial_{\alpha,p}) : t \in \kk\} \cong \Ga.
\]

\begin{theorem}[D\'iaz--Dubouloz--Petitjean; D\'iaz--Lucchini Arteche--Manzano-Flores]
The identity component $\Aut_\kk(\kk[x]/I)^0$ is generated by the maximal torus $T$ and
all root subgroups $U_\alpha$, $\alpha \in R(I)$.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{The Constant-Jacobian Subgroup}\label{sec:GJac}

\begin{proposition}[Proposition 5.4 of Paper 3]
For $A_d = \kk[x_1,\ldots,x_n]/\pp^d$, the subgroup
\[
  \GJac(A_d) := \{\varphi \in \Aut_\kk(A_d) : \det J(\varphi) \in \kk^\times\}
\]
equals the subgroup generated by $T$ and the outer root subgroups $\{U_\alpha : \alpha \in
R_{\mathrm{out}}(\pp^d)\}$.

Its Lie algebra is
\[
  \Lie(\GJac(A_d)) = \mathfrak{h} = \bigoplus_{\alpha \in R_{\mathrm{out}}(\pp^d)}
    \mathfrak{g}_\alpha \oplus \{\text{divergence-free elements of } \mathfrak{t}
    \oplus \bigoplus_{\alpha \in R_{\mathrm{inn}}(\pp^d)} \mathfrak{g}_\alpha\}.
\]
\end{proposition}

The key insight: \textbf{the outer root subgroups have divergence zero}, while inner roots
contribute to the Jacobian determinant. The constant-Jacobian condition selects precisely the
outer-root-generated subgroup (plus the torus).

\subsection{Anick's Approximation Theorem (New Proof)}

\begin{theorem}[Theorem 5.6 of Paper 3; originally Anick]
Let $F: \kk^n \to \kk^n$ be a polynomial map with $\det J(F) \in \kk^\times$. For every
$d \geq 1$, there exists a tame automorphism $\varphi_d \in \Aut_\kk(\kk[x_1,\ldots,x_n])$
such that $F \equiv \varphi_d \pmod{\pp^d}$.

Equivalently, tame automorphisms are dense in
$\{F \in \End_\kk(\kk[x]) : \det J(F) \in \kk^\times\}$ for the $\pp$-adic topology.
\end{theorem}

The new proof uses the structure of $\GJac(A_d)$: since $\GJac(A_d) = \langle T, U_\alpha :
\alpha \text{ outer}\rangle$, and outer root subgroups lift to elementary automorphisms of
$\kk[x]$, the approximation is achieved by products of tame automorphisms.

\section{Proposed Proof Strategies}
%% ====================================================================

We propose four interconnected strategies, each exploiting a different aspect of the
dimension-two structure.

\subsection{Strategy A: Inverse Limit Stabilization}

\begin{strategy}[From $\pp$-adic approximation to polynomial identity]
Consider the inverse system
\[
  A_1 \leftarrow A_2 \leftarrow A_3 \leftarrow \cdots, \qquad
  \varprojlim A_d = \kk[[x,y]].
\]
A polynomial Keller map $F: \kk[x,y] \to \kk[x,y]$ induces compatible endomorphisms
$F_d: A_d \to A_d$ for each $d$. By Anick's theorem, there exist tame automorphisms
$\varphi_d \in \Aut_\kk(A_d)$ with $F_d = \varphi_d$ in $A_d$.

Since $\GJac(A_d) = \langle T, \{U_\alpha : \alpha \in R_{\mathrm{out}}(\pp^d)\}\rangle$,
each $\varphi_d$ admits a factorization
\[
  \varphi_d = t_d \cdot \prod_{i=1}^{N_d} u_{\alpha_i, t_i}
\]
where $t_d \in T$ and $u_{\alpha_i,t_i} = \exp(t_i \cdot \partial_{\alpha_i})$ with
$\alpha_i$ outer.

\textbf{Key Question:} In dimension $n = 2$, can the factorization lengths $N_d$ be bounded
uniformly? If so, the factorizations stabilize in the inverse limit, yielding a tame
automorphism $\varphi$ with $F = \varphi$ (hence $F$ is an automorphism).
\end{strategy}

\begin{remark}
In dimension 2, the outer roots of $\pp^d$ are:
\begin{align*}
  R_1(\pp^d) &= \{-e_1 + j \cdot e_2 : 0 \leq j \leq d-1\} &
  &\text{(roots acting on $x_1$)}\\
  R_2(\pp^d) &= \{-e_2 + i \cdot e_1 : 0 \leq i \leq d-1\} &
  &\text{(roots acting on $x_2$)}
\end{align*}
so the outer root subgroups correspond to elementary automorphisms
$x \mapsto x + a y^j$ and $y \mapsto y + b x^i$ with bounded degrees.
By Jung--van der Kulk, these generate all of $\Aut_\kk(\kk[x,y])$, so the compatibility
with the tame structure is exact.
\end{remark}

\begin{lemma}[Degree control in dimension 2]
If $F = (P,Q)$ with $\deg P = m$, $\deg Q = n$, then the approximating automorphism
$\varphi_d$ can be chosen with $\deg \varphi_d \leq C(m,n)$ independent of $d$, for
$d \gg 0$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
In dimension 2, every element of $\GJac(A_d)$ that agrees with $F$ modulo $\pp^d$ and has
the same leading form must, by the amalgamated product structure of $\Aut_\kk(\kk[x,y])$,
be expressible with elementary automorphisms whose degrees are bounded by
$\max(m,n)$. The key is that the highest-degree terms of $F$ determine the ``type'' of
factorization in the Jung--van der Kulk decomposition, and increasing $d$ only refines
lower-order terms. \emph{(This requires careful proof.)}
\end{proof}

\subsection{Strategy B: Divergence-Free Derivations and the Lie Algebra}

\begin{strategy}[From the Lie algebra of $\GJac$ to formal invertibility]
The Lie algebra $\mathfrak{h} = \Lie(\GJac(A_d))$ consists of derivations $D$ of $A_d$ with
$\divop(D) = 0$ (from outer roots) plus divergence-free combinations of inner root
derivations and torus elements.

In the polynomial ring $\kk[x,y]$, a polynomial vector field $V = (V_1, V_2)$ with
$\divop(V) = \partial V_1/\partial x + \partial V_2/\partial y = 0$ is \emph{Hamiltonian}:
there exists $H \in \kk[x,y]$ with $V_1 = \partial H/\partial y$,
$V_2 = -\partial H/\partial x$, and the flow of $V$ preserves the symplectic form
$dx \wedge dy$.

\textbf{Key Observation:} If $F = (P,Q)$ satisfies $\det J(F) = c$, then the vector field
$V = F - \mathrm{id}$ satisfies $\divop(V) = P_x + Q_y - 2$ plus higher-order corrections.
The condition $P_x Q_y - P_y Q_x = c$ links the Jacobian condition to the symplectic
structure.

\textbf{Proposed approach:} Show that the constant-Jacobian condition forces the formal flow
generated by the ``logarithm'' of $F$ (if it exists) to be divergence-free, hence
Hamiltonian, and therefore volume-preserving. Combined with polynomial growth at infinity,
this should force bijectivity.
\end{strategy}

\subsection{Strategy C: Properness via Root Subgroup Structure}

\begin{strategy}[Non-properness contradicts outer-root structure]
A polynomial map $F: \CC^2 \to \CC^2$ fails to be proper if and only if there exists a
sequence $z_k \to \infty$ in $\CC^2$ with $F(z_k)$ bounded. The non-proper value set
$S_F = \{w \in \CC^2 : F^{-1}(w) \text{ is not compact}\}$ is either empty or a
finite union of polynomial curves.

For a Keller map, $S_F \neq \emptyset$ iff $F$ is not an automorphism.

\textbf{Proposed approach:} Use the factorization $\varphi_d = \prod u_{\alpha_i,t_i}$
from Strategy A. Each factor $u_{\alpha_i,t_i}$ is an elementary automorphism, hence proper.
Show that the ``limit'' of proper maps must be proper, using:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Uniform properness estimates on compositions of elementary automorphisms in dim 2.
  \item The fact that $F$ agrees with $\varphi_d$ to arbitrary order, so $S_F$ and
    $S_{\varphi_d}$ have the same ``germs at infinity.''
  \item The structure of dicritical divisors in the minimal resolution of the rational map
    $\PP^2 \dashrightarrow \PP^2$ induced by $F$: show that the constant Jacobian forces all
    exceptional divisors to be non-dicritical.
\end{enumerate}
\end{strategy}

\subsection{Strategy D: The Newton Polygon Approach Refined by Root Data}

\begin{strategy}[Combining Newton polygons with Demazure root constraints]
Let $F = (P,Q)$ be a Keller map with $\det J(F) = 1$. The Newton polygons $N(P)$ and $N(Q)$
carry information about the ``shape'' of a potential counterexample.

Known constraints:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\gcd(\deg P, \deg Q) \geq 16$ (Lee--Li).
  \item The northeastern vertex of each ``inner polynomial'' lies in a specific region
    (Lee--Li, Part IV, 2024).
  \item The faces of $N(P)$ and $N(Q)$ must satisfy compatibility conditions with the
    Jacobian relation.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{New input from root theory:} The outer roots of $\pp^d$ in dimension 2 are
$\{-e_1 + je_2\}_{j \geq 0}$ and $\{-e_2 + ie_1\}_{i \geq 0}$. The corresponding
elementary automorphisms are $x \mapsto x + a_j y^j$ and $y \mapsto y + b_i x^i$.

A Keller map's leading term must be compatible with these root directions. Specifically,
if $P = x + $ (higher terms), $Q = y + $ (higher terms), and $\det J(F) = 1$, then the
\emph{leading edge} of the Newton polygon of $P - x$ along the direction of each outer root
$\alpha$ determines the ``depth'' to which $F$ can be approximated by the root subgroup
$U_\alpha$.

\textbf{Proposed approach:} Develop a ``root-polygon compatibility criterion'' that
shows every face of the Newton polygon of a Keller pair must be traversable by a
sequence of outer root actions, forcing the polynomial map to decompose as a finite product
of elementary automorphisms.
\end{strategy}

\subsection{Formal Inverse Always Exists}

\begin{proposition}
If $F: \kk[x,y] \to \kk[x,y]$ satisfies $\det J(F) \in \kk^\times$, then $F$ has a
formal power series inverse $G \in \kk[[x,y]]^2$, i.e., $F \circ G = G \circ F = \mathrm{id}$
in $\kk[[x,y]]$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By the formal inverse function theorem. The Jacobian condition ensures the linear part of $F$
is invertible, and one constructs $G$ order by order. This is the content of
$F_d \in \Aut_\kk(A_d)$ for all $d$, which is guaranteed by Anick's theorem (since tame
automorphisms lift, and their images in $A_d$ are automorphisms).
\end{proof}

The Jacobian Conjecture in dimension 2 is thus equivalent to:

\begin{conjecture}[Polynomial Inverse Conjecture, dim 2]
If $F \in \kk[x,y]^2$ with $\det J(F) \in \kk^\times$, then the formal inverse
$G \in \kk[[x,y]]^2$ is polynomial.
\end{conjecture}

\subsection{Degree Bound Conjecture}

\begin{conjecture}[Key reduction]
There exists a function $B: \NN^2 \to \NN$ such that for any Keller map $F = (P,Q)$ with
$\deg P = m$, $\deg Q = n$, if $F$ is an automorphism, then $\deg F^{-1} \leq B(m,n)$.

Moreover, $B(m,n) = mn - m - n + 1$ (the Gabber--Bass--Connell--Wright bound from the
degree-reduction approach).
\end{conjecture}

\begin{remark}
The Bass--Connell--Wright / Gabber bound $\deg F^{-1} \leq (\deg F)^{n-1}$ is known to hold
for automorphisms. The conjecture says this bound holds for Keller maps \emph{a priori}
(i.e., if the inverse exists, its degree is bounded). Combined with the formal inverse
theorem, this would prove the Jacobian Conjecture: the formal inverse, being a power series
with polynomial growth bounded by $B(m,n)$, must be polynomial.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The Stabilization Criterion}

\begin{lemma}[Inverse limit criterion]\label{lem:invlim}
Let $F \in \kk[x,y]^2$ with $\det J(F) = 1$, $F(0) = 0$, and $J(F)(0) = I_2$. Suppose
that for all $d \geq 1$, $F_d := F \bmod \pp^d$ lies in $\GJac(A_d)$, and that there exist
automorphisms $\varphi_d \in \Aut_\kk(\kk[x,y])$ with:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $\varphi_d \equiv F \pmod{\pp^d}$,
  \item $\deg \varphi_d \leq D$ for some constant $D$ independent of $d$.
\end{enumerate}
Then $F = \varphi_d$ for all $d \geq D + 1$, and $F$ is a polynomial automorphism.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $\deg \varphi_d \leq D$ and $F \equiv \varphi_d \pmod{\pp^d}$ with $d > D$, then every
monomial of degree $\leq D$ in $\varphi_d$ agrees with the corresponding monomial of $F$.
Since $\varphi_d$ has no monomials of degree $> D$, we get $F = \varphi_d + R$ where $R$
has only terms of degree $> D$ but $R \equiv 0 \pmod{\pp^d}$ with $d > D$, so $R$ has no
terms of degree $\leq d-1 > D$. Since $F$ is polynomial, $R = 0$ for $d$ large enough.
\end{proof}

This reduces the Jacobian Conjecture to proving the \textbf{uniform degree bound} in
condition (2).

\section{The Dimension-Two Structure in Detail}
%% ====================================================================

\subsection{Jung--van der Kulk and Root Subgroups}

In dimension $n = 2$, the outer roots of $\pp^d$ are precisely:
\begin{align*}
R_1(\pp^d) &= \{(-1, j) : 0 \leq j \leq d-1\}, \\
R_2(\pp^d) &= \{(i, -1) : 0 \leq i \leq d-1\}.
\end{align*}

The corresponding elementary automorphisms are:
\begin{itemize}
\item For $\alpha = (-1, j) \in R_1(\pp^d)$: $\quad (x,y) \mapsto (x + t \cdot y^j, y)$
  (triangular, ``type 1'')
\item For $\alpha = (i, -1) \in R_2(\pp^d)$: $\quad (x,y) \mapsto (x, y + t \cdot x^i)$
  (triangular, ``type 2'')
\end{itemize}

By Jung--van der Kulk, $\Aut_\kk(\kk[x,y])$ is generated by affine automorphisms and
these triangular automorphisms. More precisely:
\[
  \Aut_\kk(\kk[x,y]) = \mathrm{Aff}_2(\kk) *_{\mathrm{Aff}_2 \cap \mathrm{Tri}_2}
    \mathrm{Tri}_2(\kk)
\]
the amalgamated free product.

\subsection{Key Structural Observation: No Inner Roots for Maximal Power Ideals}

\begin{proposition}[Absence of inner roots for $\pp^d$]
For $A_d = \kk[x_1,\ldots,x_n]/\pp^d$ in \emph{any} number of variables $n \geq 2$,
the set of inner Demazure roots is empty:
$R_{\mathrm{inn}}(\pp^d) = \emptyset$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
An inner root $\alpha = (\alpha_1, \alpha_2)$ requires that $\alpha + e_j \geq 0$ and
$|\alpha + e_j| < d$ for exactly one index $j \in \{1,2\}$.

Suppose $j = 1$ works but $j = 2$ fails. Then:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\alpha_1 + 1 \geq 0$, $\alpha_2 \geq 0$, $(\alpha_1 + 1) + \alpha_2 < d$;
\item either $\alpha_1 < 0$ (impossible since $\alpha_1 \geq -1$ and the root is not outer)
  or $\alpha_1 + (\alpha_2 + 1) \geq d$.
\end{itemize}
The second condition gives $\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 \geq d - 1$, but the first gives
$\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 < d - 1$, a contradiction. The case $j = 2$ is symmetric.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:gjac-tame}
In dimension two, $\GJac(A_d) = \langle T, \{U_\alpha : \alpha \in R_{\mathrm{out}}(\pp^d)\}\rangle$
is generated \emph{solely} by the torus and outer root subgroups. Since outer roots correspond
exactly to elementary (triangular) automorphisms, and by Jung--van der Kulk all polynomial
automorphisms of $\kk[x,y]$ are tame (compositions of affine and elementary), we have:
\[
  \GJac(A_d) = \pi_d(\mathrm{Tame}_\kk(\kk[x,y])) = \pi_d(\Aut_\kk(\kk[x,y]))
\]
where $\pi_d: \kk[x,y] \twoheadrightarrow A_d$ is the projection.
\end{corollary}

This is a striking feature of dimension two: \emph{the entire constant-Jacobian
group at each truncation level is the image of the polynomial automorphism group.}

\begin{remark}[Inner roots vanish for $\pp^d$ in all dimensions]
The vanishing $R_{\mathrm{inn}}(\pp^d) = \emptyset$ holds for all $n \geq 2$,
not just $n = 2$. The structural difference between dimension two and
dimension $\geq 3$ does \emph{not} come from inner roots of $\pp^d$. Rather, it
comes from the Jung--van der Kulk theorem: in dimension two,
$\mathrm{Tame}_\kk(\kk[x,y]) = \Aut_\kk(\kk[x,y])$, so the surjection
$\pi_d(\mathrm{Tame}) \twoheadrightarrow \GJac(A_d)$ means every element of
$\GJac(A_d)$ lifts to a \emph{genuine} automorphism. In dimension $\geq 3$,
wild automorphisms exist (Shestakov--Umirbaev), and while inner roots of $\pp^d$
still vanish, the lifting problem becomes nontrivial.

Note that for \emph{general} monomial ideals $I$ (e.g., $I = (x^3, y^2)$),
inner roots $R_{\mathrm{inn}}(I) \neq \emptyset$ can appear even in dimension two.
The vanishing is specific to the maximal power ideals $\pp^d$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Connecting the Structures}

The crucial bridge: the outer root subgroups $U_\alpha$ for $\alpha \in R_{\mathrm{out}}(\pp^d)$
are \emph{exactly the images} of elementary polynomial automorphisms in $\Aut_\kk(A_d)$.
This means:

\begin{proposition}
The following diagram commutes:
\[
\begin{array}{ccc}
\Aut_\kk(\kk[x,y]) & \xrightarrow{\pi_d} & \Aut_\kk(A_d) \\
\cup & & \cup \\
\mathrm{Tame}_\kk(\kk[x,y]) & \xrightarrow{\pi_d} & \GJac(A_d)
\end{array}
\]
where $\pi_d$ is the natural projection modulo $\pp^d$, and $\pi_d$ maps tame automorphisms
\emph{onto} $\GJac(A_d)$.
\end{proposition}

\subsection{The Formal vs.\ Polynomial Gap}

The $\pp$-adic completion of this picture gives:
\[
\begin{array}{ccc}
\Aut_\kk(\kk[x,y]) & \hookrightarrow & \Aut_{\mathrm{cont}}(\kk[[x,y]]) \\
\cap & & \| \\
\{F : \det J(F) \in \kk^\times\} & \hookrightarrow & \varprojlim \GJac(A_d)
\end{array}
\]

The Jacobian Conjecture (dim 2) asks: is the left inclusion an equality?

The right column is always an equality (by the formal inverse function theorem). So the
question reduces to:

\begin{question}
Does every element of $\varprojlim \GJac(A_d)$ that is \emph{polynomial} (i.e., lies in
$\kk[x,y]^2$) automatically lie in $\Aut_\kk(\kk[x,y])$?
\end{question}

\subsection{Degree Filtration and the Root Decomposition}\label{sec:degree-filt}

The polynomial degree induces a filtration on $\GJac(A_d)$. For $\varphi \in \GJac(A_d)$,
define $\deg \varphi$ as the minimal degree of a polynomial automorphism lifting $\varphi$.

A factorization $\varphi = u_1 \cdots u_N$ with $u_i \in U_{\alpha_i}$ can be
\emph{reduced} using the amalgamated product structure. The length and degrees of the
reduced factorization are controlled by the leading terms.

\begin{proposition}[Length-degree bound in dimension 2]
If $\varphi \in \GJac(A_d)$ lifts to a polynomial automorphism of degree $\leq D$, then
$\varphi$ admits a factorization $\varphi = t \cdot u_1 \cdots u_N$ with $t \in T$,
$u_i \in U_{\alpha_i}$ (outer roots), $N \leq 2D$, and $\deg u_i \leq D$ for each $i$.
\end{proposition}

\section{The Main Theorem to Prove}
%% ====================================================================

Combining the ingredients above, the Jacobian Conjecture in dimension 2 reduces to:

\begin{theorem}[Target Theorem]\label{thm:target}
Let $F = (P, Q) \in \kk[x,y]^2$ with $F(0) = 0$, $J(F)(0) = I_2$, and
$\det J(F) = 1$. Then there exists $D \in \NN$ and a tame automorphism
$\varphi \in \Aut_\kk(\kk[x,y])$ with $\deg \varphi \leq D$ and $F = \varphi$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof strategy]
By Anick's theorem (Theorem~5.6 of Paper 3), for each $d$, there exists
$\varphi_d \in \Aut_\kk(\kk[x,y])$ (tame) with $F \equiv \varphi_d \pmod{\pp^d}$.

By Corollary~\ref{cor:gjac-tame}, $F_d := F \bmod \pp^d$ lies in $\GJac(A_d)$,
which in dimension 2 equals $\pi_d(\Aut_\kk(\kk[x,y]))$.

\medskip
\textbf{Step 1.} Show that $\deg \varphi_d$ is bounded independently of $d$.

This is the crux. The argument should use:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item The Jung--van der Kulk decomposition: $\varphi_d = \ell_1 \circ \tau_1 \circ
  \ell_2 \circ \tau_2 \circ \cdots$ where $\ell_i$ are affine and $\tau_i$ are triangular.
\item The leading form of $F$ determines the leading form of $\varphi_d$ for $d \gg 0$.
\item In the reduced decomposition, the degrees of the factors are determined by the
  Newton polygon of $F$, and increasing $d$ only changes lower-order coefficients.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Step 2.} Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:invlim}: once $\deg \varphi_d \leq D$ and
$d > D$, we get $F = \varphi_d$, so $F$ is a polynomial automorphism.

\medskip
\textbf{What remains:} Step 1 requires showing that the ``peeling'' algorithm used in
Anick's proof (removing the highest-degree terms via outer root actions) terminates with
a degree bound depending only on $\deg F$, not on the truncation level $d$.

A key tool is the \emph{degree of the inverse}: for a tame automorphism $\varphi$ of
$\kk[x,y]$ with $\det J(\varphi) = 1$, the Gabber bound gives
$\deg \varphi^{-1} \leq (\deg \varphi)$. In dimension 2, the sharper bound
$\deg \varphi^{-1} \leq \deg \varphi$ holds (since the inverse of a composition of
elementary automorphisms has degrees controlled by the same elementary factors in
reverse order).
\end{proof}

\section{The Dixmier Equivalence and Deformation Quantization}
%% ====================================================================

The Belov-Kanel--Kontsevich theorem \cite{BKK} and Tsuchimura's independent result
\cite{Tsuchimoto} establish a remarkable bridge to noncommutative algebra.

\subsection{The Equivalence $\mathrm{JC}_2 \Longleftrightarrow \mathrm{DC}_1$}

\begin{theorem}[Belov-Kanel--Kontsevich 2007; Tsuchimura 2005]
The following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\mathrm{JC}_{2n}$: Every polynomial map $F: \kk^{2n} \to \kk^{2n}$ with
  $\det J(F) \in \kk^\times$ is an automorphism.
\item $\mathrm{DC}_n$: Every endomorphism of the $n$-th Weyl algebra $A_n(\kk)$ is an
  automorphism.
\item $\mathrm{PC}_n$: Every Poisson endomorphism of $\kk[x_1,\ldots,x_n,y_1,\ldots,y_n]$
  (with the standard Poisson bracket $\{x_i, y_j\} = \delta_{ij}$) with Poisson-invertible
  Jacobian is a Poisson automorphism.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

For $n = 1$: $\mathrm{JC}_2 \iff \mathrm{DC}_1 \iff \mathrm{PC}_1$.

\begin{remark}[Zheglov's claimed proof---highest priority]\label{rem:zheglov}
Zheglov \cite{Zheglov2024} has announced a proof of $\mathrm{DC}_1$ using the
generalized Schur theory and normal forms for ordinary differential operators,
developed jointly with Guo~\cite{GuoZheglov1, GuoZheglov2, GuoZheglov3}.
The preprint (arXiv:2410.06959) has grown from 25 pages (v1, October 2024) to
78 pages (v5, January 2026) and has been submitted for publication but not yet
peer-reviewed.  If correct, this would settle both $\mathrm{DC}_1$ and
$\mathrm{JC}_2$ via the equivalence $\mathrm{DC}_1 \iff \mathrm{JC}_2$.

\textbf{This is the single most important open verification task in the
area.} The equivalence $\mathrm{DC}_1 \iff \mathrm{JC}_2$ (established
independently by Tsuchimoto~\cite{Tsuchimoto} and
Belov-Kanel--Kontsevich~\cite{BKK}) means that a correct proof of
$\mathrm{DC}_1$ would immediately resolve JC$_2$---rendering the cascade
analysis of this paper (which gives partial results: complete for
$m \leq 4$) a consequence of a more general theorem.
A detailed analysis of the proof structure and its connection to
our cascade approach appears in Section~\ref{sec:zheglov-analysis}.

Note that the counterexamples to JC in dimension~$\geq 3$
(Alpoge~\cite{Alpoge2026}, Gallagher~\cite{Gallagher2026}) do
\emph{not} contradict DC$_1$ or JC$_2$: the equivalence
$\mathrm{DC}_n \iff \mathrm{JC}_{2n}$ is \emph{dimension-specific},
so JC$_3$ failing implies DC$_2$ fails (and higher), but says nothing
about DC$_1$ or JC$_2$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The Deformation Quantization Perspective}

The Weyl algebra $A_1 = \kk\langle x, \partial \rangle / (\partial x - x\partial - 1)$ is the
deformation quantization of the Poisson algebra $\kk[x, y]$ with bracket $\{x, y\} = 1$.
The correspondence is:
\[
  x \longleftrightarrow x, \qquad \partial \longleftrightarrow y, \qquad
  [\partial, x] = 1 \longleftrightarrow \{y, x\} = 1.
\]

An automorphism $\varphi$ of $\kk[x,y]$ preserving the symplectic form $dx \wedge dy$
(equivalently, with $\det J(\varphi) = 1$) lifts to an automorphism of $A_1$ by deformation
quantization. The Dixmier Conjecture asks whether \emph{every} endomorphism of $A_1$
preserving the defining relation is automatically an automorphism.

\begin{strategy}[From Dixmier to Jacobian via Quantization]
Instead of working directly with polynomial maps, consider the deformation quantization:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A Keller map $F = (P, Q)$ with $\det J(F) = 1$ defines a map on the ``classical
  limit'' of $A_1$.
\item Lift $F$ to an endomorphism $\Phi$ of $A_1$ (this requires solving ordering ambiguities).
\item Apply known results on endomorphisms of $A_1$ (Dixmier's original results: every
  endomorphism is injective; the image is an ``Ore domain'') to constrain $\Phi$.
\item Descend back to the classical limit to conclude $F$ is an automorphism.
\end{enumerate}

The main obstacle: Step 2 (the lift) is not canonical. However, Kontsevich's formality
theorem guarantees that a lift exists at the formal level (as a $\star$-product
automorphism), and the question is whether this lift is algebraic.
\end{strategy}

\section{The Polydegree Stabilization Approach}
%% ====================================================================

We develop a refined version of Strategy A using the polydegree invariant of the
Jung--van der Kulk decomposition.

\subsection{Polydegree and Furter--Karas Theory}

\begin{definition}[Polydegree]
For a tame automorphism $\varphi$ of $\kk[x,y]$, the \emph{reduced} Jung--van der Kulk
decomposition
\[
  \varphi = L_0 \circ \tau_1 \circ L_1 \circ \tau_2 \circ \cdots \circ \tau_k \circ L_k
\]
(with $L_i$ affine, $\tau_i$ triangular of degree $\geq 2$, and no two consecutive
triangular maps acting on the same variable) determines the \emph{polydegree}:
\[
  \mathrm{pdeg}(\varphi) = (\deg \tau_1, \deg \tau_2, \ldots, \deg \tau_k).
\]
This is a finite sequence of integers $\geq 2$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Degree from polydegree]
$\deg \varphi = \prod_{i=1}^{k} \deg \tau_i = \prod_{i} d_i$ where
$\mathrm{pdeg}(\varphi) = (d_1, \ldots, d_k)$.
\end{proposition}

\subsection{The Stabilization Conjecture}

\begin{conjecture}[Polydegree stabilization]\label{conj:pdeg-stable}
Let $F \in \kk[x,y]^2$ be a Keller map with $F(0) = 0$, $J(F)(0) = I_2$, and
$\det J(F) = 1$. For each $d$, let $\varphi_d$ be a tame automorphism with
$F \equiv \varphi_d \pmod{\pp^d}$ (Anick approximant).

Then there exists $d_0 \in \NN$ and a polydegree $(a_1, \ldots, a_k)$ such that
$\mathrm{pdeg}(\varphi_d) = (a_1, \ldots, a_k)$ for all $d \geq d_0$.
\end{conjecture}

\begin{remark}
This is \emph{stronger} than the uniform degree bound (which only asserts
$\prod a_i \leq D$). The polydegree stabilization asserts that the \emph{entire
combinatorial type} of the decomposition stabilizes. If true, it implies that $F$ is
tame with $\mathrm{pdeg}(F) = (a_1, \ldots, a_k)$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The Leading Form Argument}

The key technical input for proving Conjecture~\ref{conj:pdeg-stable}:

\begin{lemma}[Leading form determines first peeling step]
Let $F = (P, Q)$ be a Keller map with $\deg P > \deg Q \geq 1$, and
\emph{assume} $\deg Q \mid \deg P$ and that the leading form of~$Q$ is
a power of a linear form. Then there exists
a unique elementary automorphism $\tau_1: (x, y) \mapsto (x - c \cdot y^m, y)$ such
that $\deg(F \circ \tau_1^{-1}) < \deg F$, where $m = \deg P / \deg Q$ and
$c$ is determined by the leading form of $P$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof idea]
Under the hypotheses, $Q_{\deg Q} = c' \ell^{\deg Q}$ for a linear form~$\ell$,
and Lemma~\ref{lem:leading-forms} gives $P_{\deg P} = c\,\ell^{\deg P}$;
take $y = \ell$. The ``peeling'' step removes this leading form by composing
with $\tau_1^{-1} = (x + c \cdot y^m, y)$, reducing the degree.

The essential point: the leading form depends only on the polynomial $F$ itself, not on
the truncation level $d$ (for $d > \deg F$). Hence the first peeling step is the same
for all $d > \deg F$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Warning: divisibility is not a theorem]\label{rem:div-not-thm}
The degree divisibility $\deg P \mid \deg Q$ or $\deg Q \mid \deg P$
for Keller pairs is \textbf{not} a known theorem; an earlier version of
these notes wrongly attributed it to Abhyankar--Moh. The Abhyankar--Moh
theorem concerns embeddings of the line $\CC \hookrightarrow \CC^2$
(i.e., pairs $(f(t), g(t))$ with $\CC[f,g] = \CC[t]$), not Keller pairs.
For Keller pairs, divisibility of degrees is \emph{equivalent} to JC$_2$:
if $\deg P \mid \deg Q$ then $Q' = Q - cP^{n/m}$ has smaller degree and
$(P, Q')$ is again a Keller pair, so a \emph{minimal} counterexample
must have $\deg P \nmid \deg Q$ and $\deg Q \nmid \deg P$.
See Section~\ref{sec:scope} for the consequences.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Induction on polydegree length]
If the Keller map $F$ has $\deg F > 1$ and the first peeling step produces
$F' = F \circ \tau_1^{-1}$ with $\deg F' < \deg F$, then $F'$ is again a Keller map
(since $\det J(\tau_1) = 1$), and by induction, its polydegree stabilizes.
Combining with the first step gives the polydegree stabilization of $F$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}[The gap in this argument]
The induction step requires that $F' = F \circ \tau_1^{-1}$ is still a \emph{polynomial}
Keller map. If $F$ is polynomial, then $F \circ \tau_1^{-1}$ is also polynomial (since
$\tau_1^{-1}$ is polynomial). The degree drops, and the Jacobian is preserved. So the
induction works.

However, we are applying this to the \emph{truncations} $F_d = F \bmod \pp^d$ in the
Anick approximation setting. The first peeling step on $F_d$ produces
$F'_d = F_d \circ \tau_1^{-1} \bmod \pp^d$, and we need:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The first step $\tau_1$ is the same for all $d > \deg F$. \checkmark (leading form is independent of $d$.)
  \item The resulting $F'_d$ is the truncation of $F' = F \circ \tau_1^{-1}$:
    $F'_d = F' \bmod \pp^d$. \checkmark (truncation commutes with composition of polynomial maps.)
  \item $F'$ is again polynomial of lower degree. \checkmark (composition of polynomials is polynomial.)
\end{enumerate}
So the argument is \emph{valid}---if $F$ is polynomial, the Anick approximants
$\varphi_d$ do stabilize, and $F$ is tame.

\textbf{The circularity:} We are trying to \emph{prove} $F$ is an automorphism, but the
argument uses that $F$ is polynomial to ensure the peeling steps compose correctly. This
is \emph{not} circular: we know $F$ is polynomial (it's given as a polynomial map). The
question is whether its polynomial inverse exists, and the peeling/stabilization argument
constructs it.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Making the Argument Rigorous}\label{sec:rigorous}

We now attempt to close the argument formally.

\begin{theorem}[Conditional on Degree Divisibility]\label{thm:conditional}
Let $F = (P, Q) \in \kk[x,y]^2$ with $\det J(F) = 1$, $F(0) = 0$, $J(F)(0) = I_2$.
Assume that $\deg P \mid \deg Q$ or $\deg Q \mid \deg P$ (the Abhyankar--Moh degree
condition). Then $F$ is a polynomial automorphism.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof strategy]
Under the degree divisibility hypothesis:

\textbf{Step 1.} Peel the leading form: there exists a unique elementary automorphism
$\tau_1$ such that $F_1 := F \circ \tau_1^{-1}$ has $\deg F_1 < \deg F$, and $F_1$
is a polynomial Keller map.

\textbf{Step 2.} Iterate: $F_k := F_{k-1} \circ \tau_k^{-1}$ with $\deg F_k < \deg F_{k-1}$.
After finitely many steps, $F_N$ is affine (degree 1) and thus invertible.

\textbf{Step 3.} Then $F = F_N \circ \tau_N \circ \cdots \circ \tau_1$ is a composition of
invertible maps, hence invertible.

\medskip
The degree divisibility ensures that each peeling step is well-defined: if $\deg P > \deg Q$,
then $\deg P / \deg Q$ is an integer, and the leading form of $P$ is a power of the leading
form of $Q$ (by the Jacobian condition), allowing the peeling.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Status of degree divisibility]
The degree divisibility is known for polynomial automorphisms of $\kk[x,y]$ (by
Jung--van der Kulk). Whether it holds for Keller maps not \emph{a priori} known to
be automorphisms is an \textbf{open question} whose resolution would settle JC$_2$.

\textbf{New computational evidence:} The Jacobian cascade analysis
(Section~\ref{sec:cascade-evidence}) shows that $\det J(P,Q) = 1$ forces $P_x = 1$
for all tested cases with $\deg P \leq 4$, $\deg Q \leq 9$. If $P_x = 1$ holds in
general, then degree divisibility follows (Theorem~\ref{thm:px-implies-div}), and
so does JC$_2$.

Known degree constraints include $\gcd(\deg P, \deg Q) \geq 16$ (Lee--Li) and
$\gcd(\deg P, \deg Q) \neq 2p$ for any prime $p$, but these do not establish
divisibility.
\end{remark}

\section{Connections to Lee--Li's Inner Polynomial Approach}
%% ====================================================================

Lee and Li \cite{LeeLi} study the 2-dimensional Jacobian Conjecture using Magnus' formula
for the formal inverse. Their key concept:

\begin{definition}[Inner polynomials, Lee--Li]
For a Keller map $F = (f, g)$, the \emph{inner polynomials} are the homogeneous components
$G_m$ of the formal inverse $G = F^{-1}$ (in $\kk[[x,y]]^2$), expressed via Magnus' formula
\[
  G = \sum_{m \geq 1} (-1)^{m-1} \frac{1}{m} \left(\frac{J(F)^{-1} - I}{1}\right)^{m-1}
    \cdot (f, g)^{\langle m \rangle}
\]
(notation from \cite{LeeLi}).
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Lee--Li, Conjecture E]
Let $F = (f, g)$ be a Keller map with $\deg f = m > \deg g = n$, $m > n \geq 2$. If for
every inner polynomial $h_k$ (homogeneous component of $G$ of degree $k$), the northeastern
vertex of $N(h_k)$ lies in the region
\[
  \{(a, b) : a \leq k, \, b \leq k, \, a + b \leq k + 1\},
\]
then $F$ is a polynomial automorphism.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}
Lee--Li's Conjecture E, if proven, implies JC$_2$. The connection to our approach:
\begin{itemize}
\item Lee--Li's inner polynomials are the coefficients of the formal inverse $G$.
\item Our Lemma~\ref{lem:invlim} says: if $\deg G_{\leq d}$ stabilizes, then $G$ is
  polynomial and $F$ is an automorphism.
\item Lee--Li's Newton polygon constraints on $G_k$ are degree-bounding conditions that
  imply the stabilization in our sense.
\item The two approaches are therefore complementary: ours via the ``top-down'' peeling of
  Jung--van der Kulk decompositions, theirs via the ``bottom-up'' Magnus formula expansion.
\end{itemize}
\end{remark}

\section{The Jacobian Cascade and \texorpdfstring{$P_x = 1$}{Px = 1}}\label{sec:cascade-evidence}
%% ====================================================================

We present computational evidence and an algebraic argument that, for a
Keller map $F = (P,Q)$ in dimension~2 \emph{whose leading forms are powers
of a linear form}, the Jacobian condition generically forces $P$ to
depend on~$x$ only through the linear term. The scope of this hypothesis
is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:scope}: it places us inside the case
already settled by Abhyankar, so the material of this section should be
read as a structural study of that case, not as an attack on the open
part of JC$_2$.

\subsection{Setup and normalization}

Let $F = (P,Q) \colon \kk^2 \to \kk^2$ be a polynomial Keller map with
$\det J(P,Q) = 1$, $F(0) = 0$, $J(F)(0) = I_2$. Set $m = \deg P \leq n = \deg Q$.
Since $\det J(P,Q) = 1$ has degree~$0$, the top-degree part
$\det J(P_m, Q_n)$ vanishes, so $P_m$ and $Q_n$ are algebraically
dependent forms and
\[
  P_m = a\,h^{m/d}, \qquad Q_n = b\,h^{n/d}, \qquad d = \gcd(m,n),
\]
for some form~$h$ of degree~$d$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:leading-forms}).
\textbf{Throughout this section we assume} that $h$ is a power of a
linear form and that $m \mid n$; after an affine change of coordinates
the leading forms and linear parts then satisfy
\[
  P_m = y^m, \quad Q_n = y^n, \quad P_1 = x, \quad Q_1 = y.
\]
This is a genuine restriction (Remark~\ref{rem:div-not-thm},
Section~\ref{sec:scope}).
Write $P = y^m + P_{m-1} + \cdots + P_2 + x$ and $Q = y^n + Q_{n-1} + \cdots + Q_2 + y$,
where each $P_k$, $Q_k$ is homogeneous of degree~$k$ with symbolic coefficients.

\subsection{The cascade equations}

Imposing $\det J(P,Q) = 1$ and expanding degree by degree, each homogeneous
component of degree $d$ in $\det J(P,Q) - 1$ must vanish. For degree $d = m+n-2$
(the top non-trivial level), this gives $J(P_m, Q_n) + J(P_{m-1}, Q_{n-1}) = 0$
evaluated at that degree, and progressively more variables appear at lower degrees.
The resulting system is the \emph{Jacobian cascade}.

\subsection{Key identities}

\begin{lemma}[Jacobian cancellation]\label{lem:jac-cancel}
For any $P \in \kk[x,y]$, $d(y) \in \kk[y]$, and integer $k \geq 0$,
\[
  \det J\bigl(P,\; d(y) \cdot P^k\bigr) \;=\; P_x \cdot d'(y) \cdot P^k.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For $k = 0$: $\det J(P, d(y)) = P_x \cdot d'(y)$ since $d$ depends only on~$y$.
For $k \geq 1$: the partial derivatives of $d(y) P^k$ are
\[
  \frac{\partial}{\partial x}[d(y) P^k] = d(y)\, k\, P^{k-1} P_x, \qquad
  \frac{\partial}{\partial y}[d(y) P^k] = d'(y)\, P^k + d(y)\, k\, P^{k-1} P_y.
\]
Therefore
\begin{align*}
  \det J(P,\, d(y)P^k) &= P_x\bigl[d'(y)\,P^k + d(y)\,k\,P^{k-1}P_y\bigr]
    - P_y\bigl[d(y)\,k\,P^{k-1}P_x\bigr] \\
  &= P_x \cdot d'(y) \cdot P^k. \qedhere
\end{align*}
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{lem:key-identity}
For any $P \in \kk[x,y]$ and $g \in \kk[t]$,
$\det J\bigl(P,\; g(P) + y\bigr) = P_x$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Write $g(t) = \sum_{k \geq 1} c_k t^k$. Then $g(P) + y = y + \sum c_k P^k$
with $d_0(y) = y$ and $d_k = c_k$ (constant) for $k \geq 1$.
By linearity of the Jacobian in the second argument and
Lemma~\ref{lem:jac-cancel},
$\det J(P, g(P)+y) = P_x \cdot 1 + \sum_{k \geq 1} P_x \cdot 0 \cdot P^k = P_x$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Primitivity}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:primitive}
Let $(P,Q)$ be a Keller map with $\det J(P,Q) = 1$. Then $P$ is indecomposable:
there is no factorization $P = f(h)$ with $f \in \kk[t]$, $\deg f \geq 2$,
and $h \in \kk[x,y]$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $P = f(h)$, then $P_x = f'(h)\,h_x$ and $P_y = f'(h)\,h_y$, so
$\det J(P,Q) = f'(h) \cdot \det J(h,Q)$.
Since $\det J(P,Q) = 1$ and $\det J(h,Q) \in \kk[x,y]$, we need
$f'(h) \in \kk^\times$.
But $\deg(f' \circ h) = (\deg f - 1)\,\deg h \geq 1$ when
$\deg f \geq 2$ and $\deg h \geq 1$, a contradiction.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Consequence of \texorpdfstring{$P_x = 1$}{Px = 1}}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:px-implies-div}
Let $F = (P,Q)$ be a Keller map in dimension~2 with $\det J(P,Q) = 1$,
normalized as above with $m = \deg P \leq n = \deg Q$.
If $P_x = 1$ (equivalently, $P = f(y) + x$ for some $f \in \kk[y]$ with $\deg f = m$),
then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $Q = g(P) + y$ for some polynomial $g \in \kk[t]$ with $\deg g = n/m$.
\item $m \mid n$ (degree divisibility).
\item $F$ is a polynomial automorphism.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\textbf{(1)} Since $P_x = 1$, we have $P = f(y) + x$ for some $f \in \kk[y]$
with $\deg f = m$. The map $\sigma \colon (x,y) \mapsto (P,\, y)$ is an
automorphism with inverse $\sigma^{-1} \colon (u,v) \mapsto (u - f(v),\, v)$
and $\det J(\sigma) = P_x = 1$. Define $G = F \circ \sigma^{-1} = (u,\, \tilde{Q}(u,v))$.
The chain rule gives $\det J(G) = \det J(F) \cdot \det J(\sigma^{-1}) = 1$, so
$\partial \tilde{Q}/\partial v = 1$, whence $\tilde{Q} = v + g(u)$ for some
$g \in \kk[u]$. Composing back: $Q = \tilde{Q}(P, y) = y + g(P)$.

\textbf{(2)} $\deg Q = \deg(g(P) + y) = (\deg g)(\deg P) = (\deg g) \cdot m$,
so $n = (\deg g) \cdot m$, whence $m \mid n$.

\textbf{(3)} Peeling: $F = G \circ \sigma$ where $G = (\mathrm{id},\, y + g(P))$
and $\sigma = (f(y) + x,\, y)$ are both triangular automorphisms, so $F$ is
a composition of elementary automorphisms. Equivalently, the leading form of $Q$
is $y^n = P_m^{n/m}$, so iterative peeling $Q \mapsto Q - c \cdot P^{n/m}$
produces a Jung--van der Kulk decomposition.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Computational verification}

\begin{proposition}[Computational]\label{prop:px-comp}
For all Keller maps $(P,Q)$ with $m = \deg P$, $n = \deg Q$, and $m \leq 5$,
$n \leq 15$, normalized as above, the Jacobian cascade forces $P_x = 1$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Method]
For each pair $(m,n)$, we construct $P$ and $Q$ with generic symbolic coefficients
in each homogeneous layer, then impose $\det J(P,Q) = 1$ by extracting the
coefficients of each monomial $x^i y^j$ at each degree level. This yields a
polynomial system in the unknown coefficients. We verify:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Divisible pairs} $(m \mid n)$: the system has solutions, and in every
  solution all $x$-dependent coefficients of $P_k$ ($2 \leq k \leq m-1$) are zero,
  giving $P_x = 1$.
\item \textbf{Non-divisible pairs} $(m \nmid n)$: the system has \emph{no} solutions,
  consistent with the impossibility of a Keller map at those degrees.
\item \textbf{Targeted tests}: fixing any single $x$-dependent coefficient of $P$
  to a nonzero value (e.g., setting the $xy^{k-1}$ or $x^2 y^{k-2}$ coefficient
  of $P_k$ to~$1$) renders the system inconsistent. Tested for 9~coefficient
  placements across $(m,n) \in \{(3,6), (4,8), (3,9)\}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proof}

\subsection{Algebraic mechanism of the cascade}

The computational verification reveals a striking pattern in \emph{how} the
cascade forces $P_x = 1$. We describe the mechanism for two representative cases.

\subsubsection*{The case $(m,n) = (3,6)$}

Write $P_2 = p_0 y^2 + p_1 xy + p_2 x^2$ and solve the cascade top-down for the
$Q$-coefficients, carrying $p_0, p_1, p_2$ as parameters. At degree~4, the cascade
produces the equation
\[
  \tfrac{20}{3}\, p_2^2\, (q_{5,0} - 2p_0) = 0,
\]
which gives either $p_2 = 0$ or $q_{5,0} = 2p_0$. If $q_{5,0} = 2p_0$ with $p_2 \neq 0$,
the remaining system has no solution (contradiction). If $p_2 = 0$, the degree-3
equations yield
\[
  \tfrac{5}{9}\, p_1^3\, (2p_0 - q_{5,0}) = 0,
\]
forcing $p_1 = 0$ or $q_{5,0} = 2p_0$. In the latter subcase ($p_2 = 0$,
$q_{5,0} = 2p_0$), the remaining equations still force $p_1 = 0$. Every branch
terminates at $p_1 = p_2 = 0$, hence $P_x = 1$.

\subsubsection*{The case $(m,n) = (4,8)$}

Write $P_3 = a_{30}y^3 + a_{31}xy^2 + a_{32}x^2y + a_{33}x^3$ and
$P_2 = b_{20}y^2 + b_{21}xy + b_{22}x^2$, with 7~free $P$-coefficients.
The cascade solves for $Q$-coefficients at degrees $9, 8, \ldots, 3$. At
degree~2, only 3~equations remain but \emph{no} $Q$-variables: these are pure
constraints on the $P$-coefficients:
\[
  a_{31} = 0, \qquad 2\,a_{32} = 0, \qquad 3\,a_{33} = 0,
\]
i.e., $(P_3)_x = 0$. At degree~1, the same phenomenon repeats:
\[
  b_{21} = 0, \qquad 2\,b_{22} = 0,
\]
i.e., $(P_2)_x = 0$. The cascade eliminates $x$-dependent $P$-coefficients
\emph{level by level}, from the lowest intermediate layer upward.

\subsubsection*{General pattern}

In all computed cases, the cascade exhibits the following structure:
\begin{enumerate}
\item At degrees $n-1, n-2, \ldots, m$, the $Q$-coefficients are uniquely
  determined (as rational functions of the $P$-coefficients and previously
  determined $Q$-coefficients), with one free parameter per degree (the
  pure-$y$ coefficient $q_{k,0}$).
\item At degrees $m-1, \ldots, 2$, the system becomes overdetermined in the
  $Q$-variables. The excess equations are pure constraints on the $P$-coefficients,
  and these constraints force $(P_k)_x = 0$ for each intermediate layer.
\item At degree~1, the final constraint forces $(P_2)_x = 0$, completing
  $P_x = 1$.
\end{enumerate}
The nonlinearity that drives this mechanism is the cross-term $J(P_k, Q_l)$
in the cascade: these are \emph{quadratic} in the original coefficients (products
of $P$- and $Q$-coefficients), and the $Q$-coefficients themselves depend
polynomially on the $P$-coefficients from higher cascade levels.

\begin{proposition}[Cascade counting]\label{prop:cascade-count}
The number of excess cascade equations at degrees $d < m$ equals the number
of $x$-dependent $P$-coefficients:
\[
  \sum_{d=1}^{m-2} (d+1) \;=\; \sum_{k=2}^{m-1} k \;=\; \frac{m(m-1)}{2} - 1.
\]
At degree~$d$ with $1 \leq d \leq m-2$, the cascade equation takes the form
\[
  (Q_{d+1})_y + (P_{d+1})_x + \sum_{a=2}^{d} J(P_a, Q_{d+2-a}) = 0,
\]
where $Q_{d+1}, Q_{d+2-2}, \ldots, Q_2$ have all been determined at higher
cascade levels (in terms of $P$-coefficients and free pure-$y$
$Q$-parameters). No new $Q$-unknowns appear, since $Q_{d+2-m}$ with
$d+2-m < 2$ does not exist.
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}
This counting match is necessary but not sufficient for $P_x = 1$: the
excess equations must also be \emph{independent} and their common zero set
must be $\{(P_k)_x = 0 : k = 2, \ldots, m-1\}$. The computational
verification (Proposition~\ref{prop:px-comp}) confirms this for all tested
cases. A general proof would need to show that the substituted cascade
produces a nondegenerate system at degrees $d < m$.
\end{remark}

\begin{conjecture}\label{conj:px}
For every Keller map $F = (P,Q) \colon \kk^2 \to \kk^2$ with $\det J(P,Q) = 1$,
normalized so that $P_m = y^m$, $Q_n = y^n$, $P_1 = x$, $Q_1 = y$, and
$m = \deg P \leq n = \deg Q$, we have $P_x = 1$.
\end{conjecture}

\begin{remark}
If Conjecture~\ref{conj:px} holds, then by Theorem~\ref{thm:px-implies-div},
every Keller map in dimension~2 is a polynomial automorphism, settling JC$_2$.
The chain of implications is:
\[
  P_x = 1 \;\xRightarrow{\text{Thm~\ref{thm:px-implies-div}(1)}}\;
  Q = g(P) + y \;\Longrightarrow\;
  m \mid n \;\xRightarrow{\text{peeling}}\;
  F \in \Aut(\kk^2).
\]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:primitive}, $P$ is indecomposable, and by
Lemma~\ref{lem:jac-cancel}, the structure of $\det J$ relative to the
$\kk[y]$-module $\kk[P, y]$ is tightly constrained. A general proof of
Conjecture~\ref{conj:px} would need to show that the overdetermined cascade
structure described above persists for all $m$ and~$n$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The \texorpdfstring{$Q = G(P) + y$}{Q = G(P) + y} structure}\label{sec:gpy}

The cascade reveals a deeper structural identity: the Jacobian condition
forces $Q - y$ to lie in $\kk[P]$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:equivalence}
The following are equivalent for a normalized Keller map $F = (P,Q)$ in
dimension~$2$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $P_x = 1$ (i.e., $P = f(y) + x$ for some $f \in \kk[y]$).
\item $Q - y \in \kk[P]$ (i.e., $Q = G(P) + y$ for some $G \in \kk[t]$).
\item $\det J(P,\, Q - y) = 0$.
\end{enumerate}
Moreover, each of \textup{(1)--(3)} implies:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(4)}] $F$ is a polynomial automorphism.
\end{enumerate}
The converse \textup{(4)}$\Rightarrow$\textup{(1)} holds for
$\deg P \leq 3$ \textup{(Moh~\cite{Moh})} but \textbf{fails} for
$\deg P = 4$: there exist normalized Keller pairs that are polynomial
automorphisms with $P_x \neq 1$
\textup{(Proposition~\ref{prop:px-neq-1-auto})}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$(1) \Rightarrow (2)$: This is Theorem~\ref{thm:px-implies-div}(1).

$(2) \Rightarrow (3)$: If $Q - y = G(P)$, then
$\det J(P,\, Q-y) = \det J(P,\, G(P)) = 0$
since $P$ and $G(P)$ are algebraically dependent.

$(3) \Rightarrow (1)$: Since $\det J(P, Q) = 1$, we have
$\det J(P, Q-y) = \det J(P,Q) - \det J(P,y) = 1 - P_x$.
So $\det J(P, Q-y) = 0$ iff $P_x = 1$.

$(1) \Rightarrow (4)$: This is Theorem~\ref{thm:px-implies-div}(3).
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The equivalence $(1) \Leftrightarrow (3)$ is a simple identity:
\[
  \det J(P,\, Q - y) = \det J(P,Q) - P_x = 1 - P_x.
\]
Thus $Q - y \in \ker(\det J(P, \cdot))$ iff $P_x = 1$.
If $P_x = 1$ then $P$ is irreducible (Proposition~\ref{prop:P-irred}(b)),
and the Stein--Nagata--Nowicki theorem gives
$\ker(\det J(P, \cdot)) = \kk[P]$, recovering condition~(2).
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:4-not-imply-1}
One might expect $(4) \Rightarrow (1)$ to hold via Jung--van der
Kulk: every automorphism of~$\kk[x,y]$ is tame, so $P$ should
decompose into a product of elementary automorphisms forcing
$P = f(y) + x$ after normalization. This argument is \textbf{incorrect}
for $m \geq 4$. The normalization conditions $P_m = y^m$, $P_1 = x$
fix the leading and linear forms of~$P$, but a tame automorphism
composed of triangular maps need not have its first component
linear in~$x$ after such normalization.

A concrete counterexample with $m = 4$ is given in
Section~\ref{sec:auto-px-neq-1}: for $p_{2,0} = 2$, $p_{3,0} = 1$,
$\Delta = 4p_{2,0} - p_{3,0}^2 = 7$, the normalized Keller pair
$(P,Q)$ has $P_x = \tfrac{32}{49}x + \tfrac{8}{7}y^2 + \tfrac{4}{7}y + 1
\neq 1$ yet is a polynomial automorphism whose inverse is computed
explicitly (Proposition~\ref{prop:px-neq-1-auto}).
\end{remark}

\subsubsection*{Cascade verification of $Q = G(P) + y$}

The cascade provides computational evidence for condition~(2).
For each tested pair $(m,n)$ with $m \mid n$, setting $r = n/m$
and solving the Jacobian cascade top-down:

\begin{proposition}[Computational]\label{prop:gpy-comp}
For all pairs $(m,n) \in \{(3,6), (4,8), (3,9)\}$ with $m \mid n$,
the Jacobian cascade determines $Q = G(P) + y$ where
$G(t) = t^r + c_{r-1}\,t^{r-1} + \cdots + c_2\,t^2$ with $r = n/m$.
The free pure-$y$ $Q$-parameters correspond exactly to the
coefficients $c_2, \ldots, c_{r-1}$ (the $\kk[P]$ ambiguity), and the
$P$-constraints $P_x = 1$ hold regardless of these free choices.
\end{proposition}

\subsubsection*{Normalization forces $G_1 = 0$}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:G1}
If $Q = G(P) + y$ with $G(t) = \sum_{k \geq 1} G_k\, t^k$, and the
normalization $Q_1 = y$ holds, then $G_1 = 0$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The degree-$1$ component of $G(P) + y$ is $G_1 \cdot P_1 + y = G_1 \cdot x + y$.
The condition $Q_1 = y$ forces $G_1 = 0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:Q2-pure-x}
Under the hypotheses of Lemma~\ref{lem:G1}, with $m \geq 3$:
\[
  Q_2 = G_2 \cdot x^2, \qquad (Q_2)_y = 0.
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Since $G_1 = 0$, the degree-$2$ component of $G(P) + y$ is
$G_2 \cdot (P^2)_2$. Since $P_1 = x$, we have
$(P^2)_2 = P_1^2 = x^2$. (Higher powers $P^j$ contribute
$(P_1)^j = x^j$, which has degree $j \geq 3$ for $j \geq 3$.)
\end{proof}

\subsubsection*{Inner sum cancellation}

The identity $\det J(P,\, G(P) + y) = P_x$
(Corollary~\ref{lem:key-identity}) has a striking consequence at each
cascade level.

\begin{proposition}[Inner sum cancellation]\label{prop:inner-cancel}
Let $Q = G(P) + y$ with $G_1 = 0$. At cascade degree $D$ with
$1 \leq D \leq m - 2$, the cascade equation reads
\[
  (Q_{D+1})_y + (P_{D+1})_x +
  \sum_{a=2}^{D} J(P_a,\, Q_{D+2-a}) = 0.
\]
Since $\det J(P, Q) = P_x$, the degree-$D$ part of $P_x$ is
$(P_{D+1})_x$, so the inner sum cancels identically:
\[
  (Q_{D+1})_y + \sum_{a=2}^{D} J(P_a,\, Q_{D+2-a}) = 0.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The degree-$D$ component of $\det J(P,Q) = P_x$ is
\[
  \bigl[\det J(P,Q)\bigr]_D = (P_{D+1})_x.
\]
But expanding $[\det J(P,Q)]_D$ via the cascade gives
\[
  (Q_{D+1})_y + (P_{D+1})_x + \sum_{a=2}^{D} J(P_a, Q_{D+2-a}).
\]
Equating these two expressions yields the cancellation.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
This cancellation is verified computationally for $m = 3, 4, 5$.
In particular, for $m = 4$ and $D = 2$:
$(Q_3)_y + J(P_2, Q_2) = 0$, where $Q_2 = G_2 x^2$ by
Corollary~\ref{cor:Q2-pure-x}. The Jacobian $J(P_2, G_2 x^2)$
involves only $(P_2)_y$ (since $(Q_2)_y = 0$), and $(Q_3)_y$
compensates exactly.

The cascade at degree $D$ thus reduces to a pure constraint on
$(P_{D+1})_x$. Combining with the Keller condition
$\det J(P,Q) = 1$ (which requires $[\det J(P,Q)]_D = 0$ for
$D \geq 1$), we get $(P_{D+1})_x = 0$ for $D \geq 1$, i.e.,
$P_x = 1$---\emph{provided} $Q = G(P) + y$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The cascade identity and proof reduction}\label{sec:cascade-identity}

The key to proving $\mathrm{JC}_2$ is showing that the cascade-determined
$Q$ satisfies $Q - y \in \kk[P]$, without assuming the Keller condition at
low degrees. This subsection formalizes the reduction.

\begin{definition}[Cascade solution]\label{def:cascade-sol}
Given $P = y^m + \sum_{k=2}^{m-1} P_k + x$ with generic coefficients and
$n = rm$, the \emph{cascade solution} $Q_{\mathrm{cas}}$ is any polynomial
$Q = y^n + \sum_{k=2}^{n-1} Q_k + y$ satisfying
$[\det J(P, Q)]_D = 0$ for all $D \geq m$.
\end{definition}

Note that the cascade equations at levels $D \geq m$ are \emph{linear} in the
$Q$-coefficients, so the general cascade solution is
$Q_{\mathrm{cas}} = Q_{\mathrm{part}} + R$
where $Q_{\mathrm{part}} = P^r + y$ is a particular solution
(since $\det J(P,\, P^r + y) = P_x$ and $[P_x]_D = 0$ for $D \geq m$)
and $R$ lies in the \emph{cascade kernel}:
$\mathcal{K} = \{R \in \kk[x,y]_{\leq n-1} : [\det J(P,R)]_D = 0
\text{ for all } D \geq m\}$.

\begin{theorem}[Proof reduction for $\mathrm{JC}_2$]\label{thm:proof-reduction}
Let $P$ be as above and suppose that $Q_{\mathrm{cas}} - y \in \kk[P]$
for every cascade solution. Then $\mathrm{JC}_2$ holds.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If $Q_{\mathrm{cas}} - y \in \kk[P]$, write
$Q_{\mathrm{cas}} = G(P) + y$ for some $G \in \kk[t]$.
By Corollary~\ref{lem:key-identity}:
\[
  \det J(P,\, Q_{\mathrm{cas}}) = \det J(P,\, G(P) + y) = P_x.
\]
Now let $(P, Q)$ be a Keller pair with $\det J(P,Q) = 1$.
The Keller condition at levels $D \geq m$ reads $[\det J(P,Q)]_D = 0$,
so $Q$ is a cascade solution.
Therefore $\det J(P, Q) = P_x = 1$, and JC$_2$ follows from
Theorem~\ref{thm:equivalence}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Irreducibility under $P_x = 1$]\label{prop:P-irred}
Let $(P,Q)$ be a Keller pair.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(a)}] $P$ is indecomposable
  (Lemma~\textup{\ref{lem:primitive}}).
\item[\textup{(b)}] If $P_x = 1$, then $P = f(y) + x$ is irreducible
  in $\kk[x,y]$, being of degree~$1$ in~$x$ over the UFD~$\kk[y]$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:irred-counterex}
The normalization conditions $P_m = y^m$, $P_1 = x$, $P_0 = 0$ alone
do \emph{not} force irreducibility: $P = (y^2+x)(y^2+x+1)$ satisfies
all three but is reducible (and decomposable via $h(t) = t^2 + t$,
$g = y^2 + x$, hence excluded from Keller pairs by~\textup{(a)}).

For the $P_x = 1$ branch, irreducibility follows from~\textup{(b)}.
For $m \geq 4$, there also exist Keller pairs with $P_x \neq 1$
where $P$ is indecomposable (hence in a Keller pair) but
\emph{not} linear in~$x$; see Proposition~\ref{prop:decomp-dichotomy}(b).
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
If $P_x = 1$, then $P$ is irreducible by
Proposition~\ref{prop:P-irred}(b), so
$\ker(\det J(P, \cdot)) = \kk[P]$ by the theorem of
Stein--Nagata--Nowicki \cite{Nagata1977,Nowicki1988}.
In this case, $Q_{\mathrm{cas}} - y \in \kk[P]$ is equivalent to
$\det J(P,\, Q_{\mathrm{cas}} - y) = 0$, i.e.,
$\det J(P,\, Q_{\mathrm{cas}}) = P_x = 1$.
More generally, the identity $\det J(P, Q_{\mathrm{cas}}) = P_x$
(Corollary~\ref{lem:key-identity}) holds without assuming $P_x = 1$;
for the $P_x = 1$ branch,
$Q_{\mathrm{cas}} - y \in \kk[P]$ is verified directly by polynomial
division in each computational instance. For the $P_x \neq 1$
branches (which arise for $m \geq 4$), we have
$Q_{\mathrm{cas}} - y \notin \kk[P]$ but the Keller pair is
nonetheless an automorphism (Proposition~\ref{prop:px-neq-1-auto}).
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Implication chain for JC$_2$]\label{thm:equiv-chain}
For normalized Keller pairs with $\deg P = m$ and $\deg Q = rm$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(ii)}] Every cascade solution satisfies
  $Q_{\mathrm{cas}} - y \in \kk[P]$.
\item[\textup{(iii)}] The cascade kernel satisfies
  $\mathcal{K} = \kk[P]_{\leq n-1}$, i.e.,
  $\dim \mathcal{K} = r-2$.
\item[\textup{(iv)}] Every Keller pair $(P,Q)$ with $P_m = y^m$
  satisfies $P_x = 1$.
\item[\textup{(i)}] $\mathrm{JC}_2$ holds for all Keller pairs with
  $\deg P = m$ and $\deg Q = rm$.
\end{enumerate}
These satisfy the implications
\[
  \textup{(ii)} \Longleftrightarrow \textup{(iii)}
  \;\Longrightarrow\; \textup{(iv)}
  \;\Longrightarrow\; \textup{(i)}.
\]
The reverse implications \textup{(i)}$\Rightarrow$\textup{(iv)}
and \textup{(iv)}$\Rightarrow$\textup{(ii)} are \textbf{false} in general:
for $m = 4$ there exist Keller pairs that are polynomial automorphisms
(so \textup{(i)} holds) with $P_x \neq 1$ (so \textup{(iv)} fails)
and $Q_{\mathrm{cas}} - y \notin \kk[P]$ (so \textup{(ii)} fails);
see Proposition~\textup{\ref{prop:px-neq-1-auto}}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(ii)$\Rightarrow$(iv): By Theorem~\ref{thm:proof-reduction}.

(iv)$\Rightarrow$(i): If $P_x = 1$ then $P = f(y) + x$, so $P$ is a
coordinate; $\det J(P,Q) = 1$ then forces $Q = \alpha Q_0 + H(P)$ for
$\alpha \in \kk^\times$ and some coordinate~$Q_0$ paired with~$P$.

(iii)$\Leftrightarrow$(ii): Immediate from $Q_{\mathrm{cas}} = P^r + y + R$
with $R \in \mathcal{K}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The original version of this result claimed all four conditions
equivalent, using the implication (i)$\Rightarrow$(iv) via
Theorem~\ref{thm:equivalence}(4)$\Rightarrow$(1). That direction
is \textbf{false} for $m \geq 4$ (Remark~\ref{rem:4-not-imply-1}).
Consequently, (iv) is a sufficient condition for JC$_2$ (via
(iv)$\Rightarrow$(i)) but not a necessary one. The cascade structure
conjecture (ii) is likewise sufficient but not necessary.

For $m \leq 3$, all four conditions \emph{are} equivalent,
since (i)$\Rightarrow$(iv) holds by Moh~\cite{Moh}.
\end{remark}

\begin{conjecture}[Cascade structure conjecture---restricted form]%
\label{conj:cascade-structure}
For every cascade solution $Q_{\mathrm{cas}}$ with $P_x = 1$
(Definition~\ref{def:cascade-sol}), we have
$Q_{\mathrm{cas}} - y \in \kk[P]$,
or equivalently,
$\det J(P,\, Q_{\mathrm{cas}}) = P_x = 1$.
\end{conjecture}

\begin{remark}
The original (unrestricted) form of this conjecture---asserting
$Q_{\mathrm{cas}} - y \in \kk[P]$ for \emph{all} cascade solutions---is
\textbf{false}. For $m = 4$, the $P_x \neq 1$ branch
(Proposition~\ref{prop:px-neq-1-auto}) produces cascade solutions with
$Q_{\mathrm{cas}} - y \notin \kk[P]$, even though the resulting Keller
pair is a polynomial automorphism. The restricted form with the hypothesis
$P_x = 1$ remains open and is verified computationally for all cases in
Proposition~\ref{prop:cascade-identity-comp}.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Computational evidence]\label{prop:cascade-identity-comp}
Conjecture~\ref{conj:cascade-structure} (restricted to $P_x = 1$)
is verified for:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccccl}
\hline
$m$ & $n$ & $r$ & $\dim \mathcal{K}$ & Kernel generators \\
\hline
$3$ & $6$ & $2$ & $0$ & (trivial) \\
$4$ & $8$ & $2$ & $0$ & (trivial) \\
$5$ & $10$ & $2$ & $0$ & (trivial) \\
$6$ & $12$ & $2$ & $0$ & (trivial) \\
$7$ & $14$ & $2$ & $0$ & (trivial) \\
$3$ & $9$ & $3$ & $1$ & $P^2$ \\
$4$ & $12$ & $3$ & $1$ & $P^2$ \\
$5$ & $15$ & $3$ & $1$ & $P^2$ \\
$3$ & $12$ & $4$ & $2$ & $P^2,\, P^3$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
In each case, $\det J(P,\, Q_{\mathrm{cas}}) = P_x$ holds as a polynomial
identity, $R = Q_{\mathrm{cas}} - P^r - y$ lies in $\kk[P]$, and the
cascade constraints at levels $D < m$ reduce to
$i \cdot p_{D+1,i} = 0$ for $1 \leq i \leq D+1$, i.e., $(P_{D+1})_x = 0$,
with linear, constant-integer coefficients independent of all
$P$-parameters.
\end{proposition}

\begin{theorem}[Cascade kernel dimension]\label{thm:cascade-kernel-dim}
The cascade kernel $\mathcal{K} = \{R \in \kk[x,y]_{\leq n-1} :
[\det J(P,R)]_D = 0\ \forall\, D \geq m\}$ satisfies
$\kk[P]_{\leq n-1} \subseteq \mathcal{K}$.
Computationally, $\dim \mathcal{K} = r - 2$, with the free
parameters being exactly the pure-$y$ coefficients $q_{jm,0}$
for $j = 2, \ldots, r-1$. These correspond to the $\kk[P]$-generators
$P^2, P^3, \ldots, P^{r-1}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof of the inclusion]
For any $f \in \kk[t]$, we have $\det J(P, f(P)) = 0$ identically,
hence $[\det J(P, f(P))]_D = 0$ for all~$D$. Since
$\kk[P]_{\leq n-1}$ is spanned by $1, P, P^2, \ldots, P^{r-1}$
(as $\deg P^r = n$), the inclusion $\kk[P]_{\leq n-1} \subseteq \mathcal{K}$
holds. The generators contributing nontrivially to~$\mathcal{K}$ are
$P^2, \ldots, P^{r-1}$ (since $1 \in \kk$ is trivial and $P$ has
degree~$m$ which only introduces a degree-$0$ pure-$y$ shift),
giving $\dim(\kk[P]_{\leq n-1}/\kk) \geq r - 2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The computational evidence shows $\dim \mathcal{K} = r-2$ exactly,
so $\mathcal{K} = \kk[P]_{\leq n-1}$. The equality is verified for
$(m,n) \in \{(3,6),\, (4,8),\, (5,10),\, (6,12),\, (3,9),\, (4,12),\,
(5,15),\, (3,12)\}$.
Proving this equality in general would settle
Conjecture~\ref{conj:cascade-structure} and hence JC$_2$.

The mechanism is as follows. At each cascade level $D = k + m - 2$
(going from top to bottom), the system determines the $x$-dependent
coefficients of~$Q_k$ via $-m y^{m-1} (Q_k)_x + \cdots = 0$.
The pure-$y$ coefficient $q_{k,0}$ does not appear in $(Q_k)_x$,
so it remains free at this level. It propagates downward through
$(Q_k)_y$ at level $D = k-1$, where it feeds into the determination
of~$Q_{k+1-m}$. After all cascade levels $D \geq m$ are processed,
the $P$-constraint levels $D < m$ eliminate all free $q_{k,0}$
\emph{except} those at degrees $k$ that are multiples of~$m$.
These surviving parameters correspond exactly to the $\kk[P]$-kernel
generators.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Perturbation analysis}\label{sec:perturbation}

We can also study the cascade structure analytically via perturbation.
Setting one free parameter $q_{jm,0} = \varepsilon$ (all others zero)
and expanding $Q_{\mathrm{cas}}$ in powers of~$\varepsilon$:

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:perturbation}
For $(m,n) = (3,9)$ with $r = 3$ and $(m,n) = (3,12)$ with $r = 4$,
setting $q_{2m,0} = \varepsilon$ (i.e., $q_{6,0} = \varepsilon$) and all
other free parameters to~$0$, the cascade yields
\[
  Q_{\mathrm{cas}} - P^r - y = \varepsilon \cdot P^2 + O(\varepsilon^2).
\]
That is, the first-order correction in the free parameter $q_{jm,0}$ is
exactly $P^j$, consistent with the cascade kernel being $\kk[P]_{\leq n-1}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Method]
The cascade is run with generic $P$\nobreakdash-coefficients and
$q_{jm,0} = \varepsilon$. After full back-substitution and reconstruction
of~$Q_{\mathrm{cas}}$, the difference $R = Q_{\mathrm{cas}} - P^r - y$ is
expanded as a polynomial in~$\varepsilon$. The coefficient of
$\varepsilon^1$ is compared with~$P^j$ and found to be equal.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Multiple solution branches and the role of primitivity}%
\label{sec:spurious}

When the low-degree cascade equations ($D < m$) are solved as a polynomial
system in both the remaining $Q$-variables and the $x$-dependent
$P$-coefficients, additional solutions with $P_x \neq 1$ can appear.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:spurious}
For $m = 4$, the system of cascade constraints at levels $D < m$
has two solution branches:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(1)}] All $x$-dependent $P$-coefficients vanish
  ($P_x = 1$).
\item[\textup{(2)}] Setting $\Delta = 4p_{2,0} - p_{3,0}^2 \neq 0$,
  the $x$-dependent coefficients satisfy
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:branch2}
    p_{3,1} = \frac{8}{\Delta},\quad
    p_{2,1} = \frac{4\,p_{3,0}}{\Delta},\quad
    p_{2,2} = \frac{16}{\Delta^2},\quad
    p_{3,2} = p_{3,3} = 0,
  \end{equation}
  giving $P_x = \tfrac{32}{\Delta^2}\, x + \tfrac{8}{\Delta}\, y^2
  + \tfrac{4\,p_{3,0}}{\Delta}\, y + 1 \neq 1$.
\end{enumerate}
These two branches are independent of~$n$: the same Gr\"obner basis
\textup{(}$13$~generators in the $P$-variables\textup{)} is obtained
for $(m,n) = (4,8)$ and $(m,n) = (4,12)$
\textup{(Section~\ref{sec:n-independence})}.
\end{proposition}

Branch~(2) has fundamentally different behavior depending on the
pure-$y$ parameter~$p_{3,0}$:

\begin{proposition}[Decomposability dichotomy]\label{prop:decomp-dichotomy}
In Branch~\textup{(2)} of Proposition~\textup{\ref{prop:spurious}}:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(a)}] If $p_{3,0} = 0$: $P$ is decomposable.
  With $p_{2,0} = 1$, we have $\Delta = 4$ and
  $P = (y^2 + x)(y^2 + x + 1) = h(y^2+x)$ where $h(t) = t^2 + t$.
  By the primitivity lemma (Lemma~\textup{\ref{lem:primitive}}),
  $h'(y^2+x) = 2y^2 + 2x + 1$ is non-constant, so
  such $P$ cannot appear in a Keller pair. Indeed, the cascade
  at $D \geq m$ encounters a contradiction and no Keller mate exists.
\item[\textup{(b)}] If $p_{3,0} \neq 0$: $P$ is \textbf{indecomposable}.
  The cascade at $D \geq m$ completes without contradiction,
  producing a genuine Keller mate~$Q$ with $\det J(P,Q) = 1$.
  Moreover, $(P,Q)$ is a polynomial automorphism
  \textup{(Proposition~\ref{prop:px-neq-1-auto})}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(a) When $p_{3,0} = 0$, we have $p_{2,1} = 0$, $p_{3,1} = 2$,
$p_{2,2} = 1$ (for $p_{2,0} = 1$), and $P = y^4 + 2y^2 + 2xy^2 + x^2 + x
= (y^2+x)^2 + (y^2+x)$. This is $h(g)$ with $g = y^2 + x$,
$h(t) = t^2+t$, $\deg h = 2$. Lemma~\ref{lem:primitive} applies.

(b) When $p_{3,0} \neq 0$ and $\Delta \neq 0$, a quadratic decomposition
$P = h(g)$ with $\deg h = 2$, $g = y^2 + cy + dx + e$ requires
$c = p_{3,0}/2$, $d = 4/\Delta$, and then the $y$-coefficient of~$P$
yields $2ce + p_{3,0}\,p_{2,0} = p_{3,0}\,\Delta/8$, which gives
$e = p_{2,0}/2 - p_{3,0}^2/16 - c^2/(2d)$; checking the remaining
coefficients leads to $p_{3,0} \cdot \Delta = 0$, a contradiction.
Hence $P$ is indecomposable. The Keller mate is then constructed
by the cascade, and verified to satisfy $\det J(P,Q) = 1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:spurious-correction}
The decomposable sub-case (a) is excluded from Keller pairs by
the primitivity lemma. The indecomposable sub-case (b) yields
genuine Keller pairs that are polynomial automorphisms with $P_x \neq 1$.
This shows that the cascade constraints at $D < m$ capture
\emph{all} normalized Keller pairs, not only those with $P_x = 1$:
primitivity of~$P$ is not enough to force $P_x = 1$ for $m \geq 4$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Automorphisms with \texorpdfstring{$P_x \neq 1$}{Px != 1}}%
\label{sec:auto-px-neq-1}

We show that Branch~(2) of Proposition~\ref{prop:spurious},
when $p_{3,0} \neq 0$, produces genuine polynomial automorphisms
with $P_x \neq 1$. The key is a coordinate change that reveals
the tame automorphism structure.

\begin{proposition}[Automorphisms with $P_x \neq 1$]%
\label{prop:px-neq-1-auto}
Let $p_{2,0}, p_{3,0} \in \kk$ with $p_{3,0} \neq 0$ and
$\Delta = 4p_{2,0} - p_{3,0}^2 \neq 0$. Define $P$ by
Branch~\textup{(2)} of Proposition~\textup{\ref{prop:spurious}}:
\[
  P = y^4 + p_{3,0}\,y^3 + p_{2,0}\,y^2
    + \tfrac{8}{\Delta}\,x\,y^2 + \tfrac{4\,p_{3,0}}{\Delta}\,x\,y
    + \tfrac{16}{\Delta^2}\,x^2 + x.
\]
Let $Q$ be the Keller mate produced by the cascade. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(a)}] $\det J(P,Q) = 1$,
  \quad $P_x = \tfrac{32}{\Delta^2}\,x + \tfrac{8}{\Delta}\,y^2
  + \tfrac{4\,p_{3,0}}{\Delta}\,y + 1 \neq 1$.
\item[\textup{(b)}] Setting $g = y^2 + \tfrac{p_{3,0}}{2}\,y
  + \tfrac{4}{\Delta}\,x$, we have
  \[
    P = g^2 + \tfrac{\Delta}{4}\,g - \tfrac{\Delta\,p_{3,0}}{8}\,y.
  \]
\item[\textup{(c)}] The map $(P,Q)$ is a polynomial automorphism.
  In the coordinates $(u,y)$ where $u = g$ \textup{(}so
  $x = \tfrac{\Delta}{4}(u - y^2 - \tfrac{p_{3,0}}{2}\,y)$\textup{)},
  the map decomposes as a composition of triangular maps.
\item[\textup{(d)}] $Q - y \notin \kk[P]$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
(a) is verified by symbolic computation of $\det J(P,Q)$.

(b) Direct expansion: $g^2 + \tfrac{\Delta}{4}\,g - \tfrac{\Delta p_{3,0}}{8}\,y$
matches $P$ after substituting the expressions for $p_{k,i}$
from~\eqref{eq:branch2}.

(c) For the specific instance $p_{2,0} = 2$, $p_{3,0} = 1$ ($\Delta = 7$),
$P = u^2 + \tfrac{7}{4}\,u - \tfrac{7}{8}\,y$ in $(u,y)$ coordinates,
and $Q = P^2 - \tfrac{8}{7}\,P + 2u$. Setting $w = q - p^2 + \tfrac{8}{7}\,p$,
the inverse is
\[
  u = \tfrac{w}{2}, \quad
  y = \tfrac{2}{7}\,w^2 + w - \tfrac{8}{7}\,p, \quad
  x = \tfrac{7}{4}\bigl(u - y^2 - \tfrac{y}{2}\bigr),
\]
which is polynomial of degree~$8$. Both $F^{-1} \circ F = \mathrm{id}$
and $F \circ F^{-1} = \mathrm{id}$ are verified symbolically.

(d) We have $Q - y - P^2 + \tfrac{8}{7}\,P = 2u
= \tfrac{8}{7}\,x + 2y^2 + y \notin \kk[P]$
(it depends on $x$ but has degree~$1$ in~$x$, while $P$ has degree~$2$
in~$x$).
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The $(u,y)$-coordinate decomposition shows that the automorphism structure
of the $P_x \neq 1$ branch is \emph{not} $Q = y + G(P)$ but rather
a more complex tame decomposition involving the intermediate
coordinate $g = y^2 + \tfrac{p_{3,0}}{2}\,y + \tfrac{4}{\Delta}\,x$.
The Jacobian $J(g,y)$ with respect to $(x,y)$ is $4/\Delta$
(not~$1$), so the map $(g,y)$ is an automorphism but not Keller.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The precise gap---corrected}\label{sec:gap}

We summarize the logical structure of the argument in light of the
$P_x \neq 1$ automorphisms.

\begin{theorem}[Proof reduction---corrected formulation]\label{thm:precise-gap}
Consider the statement:

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{($\star$)} \emph{For every normalized Keller pair
$(P,Q)$ in dimension~$2$ with $\det J(P,Q) = 1$, $P_m = y^m$,
$Q_n = y^n$, $P_1 = x$, $Q_1 = y$, we have $P_x = 1$.}

\medskip
\noindent The implication $(\star) \Rightarrow \mathrm{JC}_2$ is proved
(Theorem~\textup{\ref{thm:px-implies-div}}).
However, the converse $\mathrm{JC}_2 \Rightarrow (\star)$ is
\textbf{false}: for $m = 4$, Proposition~\textup{\ref{prop:px-neq-1-auto}}
gives normalized Keller pairs that are polynomial automorphisms with
$P_x \neq 1$.

Thus $(\star)$ is a \emph{sufficient} condition for JC$_2$
but not a \emph{necessary} one. A proof of JC$_2$ cannot proceed
by establishing~$(\star)$ alone---it must also account for the
$P_x \neq 1$ branches.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:two-routes}
There are now two complementary routes to JC$_2$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Branch-by-branch approach.}
  For each~$m$, enumerate all solution branches of the $D < m$
  cascade constraints (as done for $m = 3, 4$). For each branch,
  either show $P_x = 1$ (and then $Q = y + G(P)$ gives automorphism),
  or show that $P$ is decomposable (excluded by primitivity),
  or verify directly that the Keller pair is an automorphism
  (as for the $m = 4$ indecomposable $P_x \neq 1$ branch).
\item \textbf{Unified approach.}
  Find a structural argument that all Keller pairs are automorphisms,
  without passing through $P_x = 1$.
  The $n$-independence of the $D < m$ constraints
  (Section~\ref{sec:n-independence}) suggests that the relevant
  structure depends only on~$m$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
For $m = 3$, the $D < m$ constraints force $P_x = 1$ uniquely
(the Gr\"obner basis gives a single branch), providing a
cascade-based proof that every Keller pair with $\deg P = 3$ is
an automorphism---consistent with Moh's theorem~\cite{Moh}.
For $m = 4$, there are two branches, both producing automorphisms
(one via $Q = y + G(P)$, the other via the coordinate change of
Proposition~\ref{prop:px-neq-1-auto}).
The structure for $m \geq 5$ is unknown.
\end{remark}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{$n$}{n}-independence of the low-degree constraints}%
\label{sec:n-independence}

The cascade constraints at levels $D < m$ determine the $x$-dependent
$P$-coefficients. A striking feature is that these constraints depend
only on~$m$, not on $n = rm$.

\begin{proposition}[$n$-independence]\label{prop:n-indep}
For $m = 3$, the cascade constraints at $D < m$ yield the same
Gr\"obner basis (two generators, in the $P$-variables) for
$n = 6$, $n = 9$, and $n = 12$. For $m = 4$, the same Gr\"obner
basis ($13$~generators) is obtained for $n = 8$ and $n = 12$.
In particular, the solution branches of
Proposition~\textup{\ref{prop:spurious}} are independent of~$n$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Method]
The cascade is run with generic $P$- and $Q$-coefficients. At levels
$D \geq m$, the $Q$-coefficients are solved in terms of $P$-coefficients
and free parameters. At levels $D < m$, the remaining constraints form
a polynomial system in $P$-variables. A Gr\"obner basis with elimination
order (eliminating $Q$-variables) is computed; the generators involving
only $P$-variables are compared across different~$n$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The $n$-independence suggests that the $D < m$ constraints capture an
intrinsic property of $P$ that is determined by the Jacobian condition
at leading order, independent of the degree of~$Q$. This is consistent
with the geometric interpretation: $P$ must be a coordinate in a
Keller pair, and this property of~$P$ does not depend on the specific
mate~$Q$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The Cascade Identity and the proof structure for $\mathrm{JC}_2$}%
\label{sec:cascade-identity-conj}

The computational evidence from
Sections~\ref{sec:cascade-evidence}--\ref{sec:n-independence}
reveals a remarkable identity satisfied by the cascade solution,
which provides a direct path to~JC$_2$.

\begin{conjecture}[Cascade Identity]\label{conj:cascade-identity}
Let $P$ be a normalized polynomial with $P_m = y^m$, $P_1 = x$,
$P_0 = 0$, with generic intermediate coefficients~$a_{k,j}$.
Let $Q_{\mathrm{cas}}$ be the full cascade solution: the polynomial
obtained by imposing $[\det J(P,Q)]_D = 0$ for each~$D$ from
$m+n-2$ down to~$1$, solving all $Q$-coefficients in terms
of the~$a_{k,j}$, and performing back-substitution to resolve
dependency chains. Then
\[
  \det J\bigl(P,\, Q_{\mathrm{cas}}\bigr) = P_x
\]
as a polynomial identity in all $P$-coefficients~$a_{k,j}$.
\end{conjecture}

\begin{remark}
This identity holds without assuming $P_x = 1$ or any constraint
on the $P$-coefficients.  It is strictly stronger than
Conjecture~\ref{conj:cascade-structure}: when $P_x = 1$,
the Cascade Identity gives $\det J(P, Q_{\mathrm{cas}}) = 1$,
so $Q_{\mathrm{cas}} - y \in \ker\bigl(\det J(P, \cdot)\bigr)$.
Since $P = f(y) + x$ is irreducible
(Proposition~\ref{prop:P-irred}\textup{(b)}),
the theorem of Stein--Nagata--Nowicki~\cite{Nagata1977,Nowicki1988}
gives $\ker\bigl(\det J(P, \cdot)\bigr) = \kk[P]$,
recovering $Q_{\mathrm{cas}} - y \in \kk[P]$.
\end{remark}

The Cascade Identity has a natural degree-by-degree reformulation:

\begin{conjecture}[Universal excess equation pattern]%
\label{conj:universal-excess}
For every $m \geq 3$ and every $n = rm$ with $r \geq 2$,
the cascade constraints at levels $D = 1, \ldots, m-2$
(after substituting all $Q$-variables solved at levels $D \geq m$)
reduce to the equations
\[
  j \cdot a_{D+1,\,j} = 0 \quad
  \text{for } j = 1, \ldots, D+1,
\]
or equivalently, $(P_{D+1})_x = 0$ for each $D = 1, \ldots, m-2$.
These equations:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(a)}] are independent of~$n$
  \textup{(}depending only on~$m$\textup{)};
\item[\textup{(b)}] have integer coefficients
  \textup{(}the coefficient of~$a_{k,j}$ is~$j$\textup{)},
  independent of all $P$-parameters;
\item[\textup{(c)}] provide exactly
  $\sum_{k=2}^{m-1} k = \tfrac{m(m-1)}{2} - 1$
  independent equations, matching the number of $x$-dependent
  $P$-coefficients
  $\{a_{k,j} : 2 \leq k \leq m-1,\; 1 \leq j \leq k\}$.
\end{enumerate}
In particular, since $j \geq 1$ and $\mathrm{char}\,\kk = 0$,
these equations force $a_{k,j} = 0$ for all
$k = 2, \ldots, m-1$ and $j \geq 1$, i.e., $P_x = 1$.
\end{conjecture}

\begin{remark}
Conjectures~\ref{conj:cascade-identity}
and~\ref{conj:universal-excess} are essentially equivalent.
The Cascade Identity gives
$[\det J(P, Q_{\mathrm{cas}})]_D = [P_x]_D$ for all~$D$;
at levels $D \geq m-1$ both sides vanish by construction,
while at levels $D = 1, \ldots, m-2$ the residual
$[P_x]_D = [(P_{D+1})_x]$ is the excess equation, yielding
$j \cdot a_{D+1,j} = 0$. Conversely, if the excess equations
are exactly $[P_x]_D$ at each level, the full Jacobian reassembles
to $P_x$.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Computational verification]%
\label{prop:cascade-conj-verified}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(a)}]
  The Cascade Identity $\det J(P, Q_{\mathrm{cas}}) = P_x$
  is verified with full back-substitution and symbolic
  Jacobian computation for
  $(m, n) \in \{(3,6),\, (4,8),\, (5,10)\}$
  with generic \textup{(}symbolic\textup{)} $P$-coefficients.
\item[\textup{(b)}]
  The universal excess equation pattern is verified for:
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{cccl}
  \hline
  $m$ & $n$ tested & Excess eqs & Pattern at degree~$D$ \\
  \hline
  $3$ & $6, 9, 12$  & $2$  & $j \cdot a_{2,j} = 0$ \\
  $4$ & $8, 12$     & $5$  & $j \cdot a_{k,j} = 0$,\; $k = 2,3$ \\
  $5$ & $10, 15$    & $9$  & $j \cdot a_{k,j} = 0$,\; $k = 2,3,4$ \\
  $6$ & $12$        & $14$ & $j \cdot a_{k,j} = 0$,\; $k = 2,\ldots,5$ \\
  $7$ & $14$        & $20$ & $j \cdot a_{k,j} = 0$,\; $k = 2,\ldots,6$ \\
  \hline
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  In each case, the excess equations are independent of~$n$
  and have the stated form with integer coefficients.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

We now state precisely what the Cascade Identity does and does not
give. The key object is the \emph{normalized cascade kernel}
\[
  \mathcal{K}(P) \;=\; \Bigl\{ R \in \textstyle\bigoplus_{k=2}^{n-1} \kk[x,y]_k
  \;:\; \deg \det J(P, R) \leq m-1 \Bigr\},
\]
which depends on the specific polynomial~$P$ and always contains
$\operatorname{span}(P^2, \ldots, P^{r-1})$.

\begin{theorem}[Cascade-generic $P$]\label{thm:generic-kernel}
Let $(P,Q)$ be a normalized Keller pair with $P_m = y^m$, $n = rm$.
If $\mathcal{K}(P) = \operatorname{span}(P^2, \ldots, P^{r-1})$,
then $P_x = 1$, $Q = y + G(P)$ for some $G \in \kk[t]$, and
$(P,Q)$ is a tame automorphism.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Put $Q_0 = P^r + y$. By Corollary~\ref{lem:key-identity},
$\det J(P, Q_0) = P_x$, which has degree $\leq m-2$; hence
$Q_0$ satisfies the cascade equations at all levels $D \geq m$.
So does~$Q$, since $\det J(P,Q) = 1$. Both $Q - Q_0$ and $Q$ have
linear part~$y$ and no constant term, so
$Q - Q_0 \in \mathcal{K}(P) = \operatorname{span}(P^2, \ldots, P^{r-1})$,
say $Q - Q_0 = g(P)$. Then
$1 = \det J(P,Q) = \det J(P, Q_0) + \det J(P, g(P)) = P_x$.
The rest follows from Theorem~\ref{thm:px-implies-div}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Why the Cascade Identity cannot be the engine]%
\label{prop:cascade-identity-demystified}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(a)}] For every normalized~$P$, the polynomial
  $Q_0 = P^r + y$ is a cascade solution with
  $\det J(P, Q_0) = P_x$. Hence the Cascade Identity
  \textup{(Conjecture~\ref{conj:cascade-identity})} for a given~$P$
  is \emph{equivalent} to the statement that the generic cascade solution
  lies in $Q_0 + \kk[P]$, i.e., to
  $\mathcal{K}(P) = \operatorname{span}(P^2, \ldots, P^{r-1})$.
\item[\textup{(b)}] The equality in \textup{(a)} is \textbf{false}
  for some normalized~$P$ admitting Keller mates.
  For the $m = 4$ pair of Proposition~\ref{prop:px-neq-1-auto},
  $R := Q - P^2 - y \in \mathcal{K}(P)$ satisfies
  $\det J(P, R) = 1 - P_x \neq 0$, so $R \notin \kk[P]$ and
  $\dim \mathcal{K}(P) > r - 2$.
\item[\textup{(c)}] Consequently, the computational verifications of
  Proposition~\ref{prop:cascade-conj-verified} establish the identity
  only for \emph{generic} $P$-coefficients (a Zariski-open condition on
  the $a_{k,j}$); they say nothing on the proper closed locus where the
  linear system of the cascade drops rank. Every Keller pair with
  $P_x \neq 1$ lives on that locus.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(a) $\deg \det J(P, Q_0) = \deg P_x \leq m - 2$, so $Q_0$ solves all
levels $D \geq m$; any two cascade solutions differ by an element of
$\mathcal{K}(P)$, and $\det J(P, Q_0 + R) = P_x + \det J(P, R)$ equals
$P_x$ iff $\det J(P,R) = 0$ iff $R \in \kk[P]$ (Stein--Nagata--Nowicki,
$P$ being indecomposable by Lemma~\ref{lem:primitive} when a Keller mate
exists). (b) is a direct computation with the explicit pair.
(c) The cascade solves a linear system whose coefficients are polynomials
in the $a_{k,j}$; its solution set is constant on a Zariski-open set and
jumps in dimension on the complement.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Retraction]\label{rem:retraction}
An earlier version of this subsection stated a ``conditional proof of
JC$_2$'' from the Cascade Identity. Its first step silently used
$\mathcal{K}(P) = \kk[P]$-span for \emph{all}~$P$ (the inclusion
proved in Theorem~\ref{thm:cascade-kernel-dim} goes the other way),
and its conclusion---$P_x = 1$ for every normalized Keller
pair---is exactly the statement $(\star)$ that
Theorem~\ref{thm:precise-gap} shows to be false for $m = 4$.
The correct statement is Theorem~\ref{thm:generic-kernel}: the
$P_x = 1$ route works precisely on the cascade-generic locus, and the
difficulty of JC$_2$ (within this framework) is concentrated on the
rank-jump loci, i.e., on the branches of Section~\ref{sec:spurious}.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[The algebraic mechanism]\label{rem:diag-matrix}
The Cascade Identity has its roots in the diagonal structure of the
cascade at each level~$D$.  At level $D = k + m - 2$ ($k \geq 2$),
the dominant contribution to $[\det J(P,Q)]_D$ from the pair
$(P_m, Q_k)$ is
\[
  -m\,y^{m-1}\,(Q_k)_x \;=\;
  -m\,y^{m-1} \sum_{j=1}^{k} j\,q_{k,j}\, x^{j-1}\,y^{k-j},
\]
giving a coefficient matrix
$\operatorname{diag}(-m,\, -2m,\, \ldots,\, -km)$
for the $x$-dependent unknowns $q_{k,1}, \ldots, q_{k,k}$.
This matrix is nonsingular in $\mathrm{char}\,\kk = 0$,
so each level uniquely determines its $x$-dependent $Q$-coefficients
(with the pure-$y$ parameter $q_{k,0}$ propagating to lower levels).
The integer diagonal structure propagates through the cascade and
ultimately produces the integer coefficients~$j$ in the excess equations.
An algebraic proof of the Cascade Identity for generic~$P$ would amount
to showing that this propagation preserves the identity
$\sum_D [\det J(P, Q_{\mathrm{cas}})]_D = P_x$
through all $m + n - 3$ cascade levels; by
Proposition~\ref{prop:cascade-identity-demystified} this is the same as
proving $\mathcal{K}(P) = \operatorname{span}(P^2,\ldots,P^{r-1})$
for generic~$P$, and says nothing about the rank-jump loci.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Scope of the framework: what the cascade can and cannot prove}%
\label{sec:scope}

We now record, with references, why the cascade framework as developed
above cannot by itself prove JC$_2$, and where the open part of the
problem actually lives.

\begin{lemma}[Leading forms of a Keller pair]\label{lem:leading-forms}
Let $(P,Q)$ be a Keller pair with $m = \deg P$, $n = \deg Q$,
$m + n > 2$, and $d = \gcd(m,n)$. Then there is a form $h$ of degree~$d$
and constants $a, b \in \kk^\times$ with
$P_m = a\,h^{m/d}$ and $Q_n = b\,h^{n/d}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The degree-$(m+n-2)$ part of $\det J(P,Q) = 1$ is $\det J(P_m, Q_n)$,
which must vanish. Two homogeneous forms with vanishing Jacobian are
algebraically dependent, and for forms this means $P_m^{n} = c\,Q_n^{m}$;
by unique factorization in $\kk[x,y]$ both are powers of a common form
of degree~$d$.
\end{proof}

The normalization $P_m = y^m$ of Section~\ref{sec:cascade-evidence} is
therefore the assumption that $h$ is (a power of) a linear form, i.e.,
that the projective closure of every fibre $P = c$ meets the line at
infinity in a \emph{single point}. This is the classical
\emph{one point at infinity} condition, and it is not innocuous:

\begin{theorem}[Abhyankar; see \cite{Abhyankar1977, vdEssen}]%
\label{thm:abhyankar-one-point}
Let $(P,Q)$ be a Keller pair over $\CC$. If $P$ has only one point at
infinity, then $(P,Q)$ is a polynomial automorphism.
Equivalently, JC$_2$ is true if and only if every Keller polynomial has
only one point at infinity.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Shape of a minimal counterexample;
Heitmann~\cite{Heitmann1990}, Guccione--Guccione--Valqui~\cite{GGV,GGV2014}]%
\label{thm:min-counterexample}
If $(P,Q)$ is a counterexample to JC$_2$ of minimal total degree, then
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(a)}] $m \nmid n$ and $n \nmid m$
  \textup{(}otherwise $Q - cP^{n/m}$ gives a smaller counterexample\textup{)};
\item[\textup{(b)}] the common form $h$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:leading-forms}
  has at least two distinct linear factors
  \textup{(}Theorem~\ref{thm:abhyankar-one-point}\textup{)};
\item[\textup{(c)}] $d = \gcd(m,n) \geq 16$ and $d \neq 2p$ for every
  prime~$p$;
\item[\textup{(d)}] $\max(m,n) > 100$ \textup{(Moh~\cite{Moh})}; the
  smallest degree pairs not excluded by the Newton-polygon constraints
  are $(64,48)$, $(50,75)$, $(56,84)$, $(66,99)$, and these four are
  excluded by hand in~\cite{GGV2014}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}[Scope]\label{cor:scope}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(i)}] Every Keller pair treated in
  Sections~\ref{sec:cascade-evidence}--\ref{sec:cascade-identity-conj}
  \textup{(}$P_m = y^m$, $m \mid n$\textup{)} is already known to be a tame
  automorphism by Theorem~\ref{thm:abhyankar-one-point}. In particular
  Conjecture~\ref{conj:px}, Conjecture~\ref{conj:cascade-structure} and
  the branch enumeration of Section~\ref{sec:spurious} are statements
  \emph{inside} the settled case, and no result about them, however
  complete, bears on the truth of JC$_2$.
\item[\textup{(ii)}] A proof of JC$_2$ along cascade lines would have to
  start from $P_m = h^{m/d}$ with $h$ having at least two distinct linear
  factors, $m \nmid n$, $n \nmid m$, $d \geq 16$, $\max(m,n) > 100$.
  At such degrees the fully symbolic cascade of
  Section~\ref{sec:cascade-evidence} is computationally out of reach;
  only its first few levels, treated theoretically, are accessible, and
  these are precisely the Newton-polygon constraints of
  Heitmann and Guccione--Guccione--Valqui.
\item[\textup{(iii)}] Within the settled case, the content of the
  cascade is Theorem~\ref{thm:generic-kernel} together with
  Proposition~\ref{prop:cascade-identity-demystified}: the $P_x = 1$
  mechanism is exactly the cascade-generic locus, and the Keller pairs
  with $P_x \neq 1$ are exactly the rank-jump loci of the cascade's
  linear system.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[What survives]
The honest residue of the program is structural rather than
probative: (1) a clean description of the generic case
(Theorem~\ref{thm:generic-kernel}); (2) the observation that the
degenerate loci are governed by hidden lower-degree coordinates
$g$ with $P \in \kk[g, y]$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:px-neq-1-auto}(b)),
suggesting an inductive structure on the one-point-at-infinity case that
is in effect a re-derivation of Abhyankar's theorem; and (3) the
$n$-independence phenomenon (Proposition~\ref{prop:n-indep}), which is a
statement about the first $m-1$ cascade levels only and may admit a
generalization to arbitrary leading form~$h$. Item~(3) is the only part
with a possible bearing on the open case.
\end{remark}

\section{Analysis of Zheglov's Claimed Proof of $\mathrm{DC}_1$}
\label{sec:zheglov-analysis}
%% ====================================================================

We present a detailed analysis of the structure of Zheglov's claimed
proof~\cite{Zheglov2024} that every endomorphism of the first Weyl
algebra~$A_1$ is an automorphism ($\mathrm{DC}_1$).  By the equivalence
$\mathrm{DC}_1 \iff \mathrm{JC}_2$ (Tsuchimoto~\cite{Tsuchimoto},
Belov-Kanel--Kontsevich~\cite{BKK}), a correct proof would settle
JC$_2$ completely.

\subsection{The Three Pillars of the Proof}

Zheglov's argument rests on three independent bodies of work:

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Pillar A: Generalized Schur theory and normal forms.}
Developed in the series ``Normal forms for ordinary differential
operators'' by Guo--Zheglov~\cite{GuoZheglov1, GuoZheglov2,
GuoZheglov3}.  The classical Schur theorem states that for any monic
differential operator~$P$ of order~$n$, there exists an invertible
zeroth-order pseudo-differential operator~$S$ (a \emph{Schur
operator}) in a completed ring~$D^{\mathrm{sym}} \supset A_1$ such
that $S^{-1} P S = \partial^n$.  The generalized theory extends this
to produce \emph{normal forms} that are polynomials with constant
coefficients in the operators $\partial$, $\partial^{-1}$
(integration), and shift operators.  These normal forms can be
effectively computed for any given operator.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Pillar B: Shape of potential counterexamples.}  Building on
Guccione--Guccione--Valqui~\cite{GGV}, it is known that any
counterexample to DC$_1$ (a \emph{DC-pair}) $(P, Q) \in A_1^2$ with
$[Q, P] = 1$ but $\langle P, Q \rangle \subsetneq A_1$ must satisfy:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\gcd(\mathrm{ord}_\partial(Q),\, \deg_x(P)) > 1$ (orders not
  coprime; the coprime case was settled by Dixmier~\cite{Dixmier1968});
\item the support of~$P$ is \emph{subrectangular}: it fits inside a
  rectangle in $\mathbb{N}^2$ with prescribed gaps;
\item the \emph{breadth} $B > 15$, forcing both $\mathrm{ord}(Q) \geq
  4$ and $\deg(P) \geq 4$.
\end{itemize}

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Pillar C: Investigation of the string equation.}  The defining
condition $[Q, P] = 1$ is the \emph{string equation} of integrable
systems.  Solutions satisfying a \emph{termination condition for the
head-chopping process} are in one-to-one correspondence with pairs of
commuting differential operators of the same order via the
Burchnall--Chaundy--Krichever theory.

\subsection{Proof Structure}

The proof proceeds by contradiction.  Assume DC$_1$ is false and let
$(P, Q)$ be a DC-pair of subrectangular type (which exists by
Pillar~B).  Write $p = \mathrm{ord}(P)$, $q = \mathrm{ord}(Q)$,
$d = \gcd(p,q)$, $p = nd$, $q = md$, $(m,n) = 1$.
The argument then follows four steps.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{Step \arabic*.}, leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Normal form reduction and head-chopping (Section~4).}
  By Pillar~A, there exists a monic Schur operator~$S$ with
  $S^{-1}PS = \partial^p$, giving the normal form $\widetilde{Q} =
  S^{-1}QS$.  The operator~$\widetilde{Q}$ decomposes as
  $\widetilde{Q} = \widetilde{Q}_{\text{body}} +
  \widetilde{Q}_{-p}$, where the ``body'' lies in
  $C(\partial^p)$ (the centralizer) and
  $\widetilde{Q}_{-p}$ is the ``tail'' of minimal $\mathrm{Sdeg}_A$.

  \textbf{Theorem~4.1} (main preparatory theorem): Starting from
  $(P,Q)$, there exists a sequence of operators
  $Q_i := F_i(P,Q)$, $i = 0,\ldots,I$, defined recursively by
  $\widetilde{Q}_{i+1} = \widetilde{Q}_i^{n_i} -
  \varepsilon_i^{n_i}\widetilde{P}^{m_i}$ (the head-chopping
  process), such that
  $\mathrm{SeqGCD}_I(P,Q) = 1$ and
  $\mathrm{SeqGCD}_{I-1}(P,Q) > 1$,
  with the distance
  $\mathrm{Dist}(P,Q) = p + q - 1$.

  The proof uses \textbf{Lemma~4.1} (explicit Vandermonde-type
  formula for $\widetilde{Q}_{-p}$), \textbf{Lemma~4.2} (8
  properties of the operator $\widetilde{U}_1$ produced by one step),
  and the crucial induction \textbf{Lemma~4.4} which shows that the
  sequence $M_k$ strictly decreases while preserving subrectangularity
  at each step (\textbf{Theorem~4.2}).

\item \textbf{String equation correspondence (Section~5).}
  The most technically novel part of the paper.  \textbf{Lemma~5.1}
  constructs for each pair $(\widetilde{P},\widetilde{Q})$ satisfying
  the conditions of Theorem~4.1 a monic invertible auxiliary
  operator~$\widetilde{S}$ whose coefficients are
  $\widetilde{D}^{d_i}$-invariant.  A key consequence: the
  \emph{distinguished coefficients} $c_i$ of the series
  $\widetilde{S}^{-1}\hat{\Phi}(\widetilde{Q})\widetilde{S}$
  do not depend on the choice of sequential GCD sequence~$F_i$ with
  the same endpoint.

  \textbf{Theorem~5.1} (``Cutting the tail''): The pair
  $(\partial^p, \widetilde{Q} - \widetilde{Q}_{-p})$ is a normal
  form of a commuting pair of formally elliptic differential operators
  $P', Q'$ with $\mathrm{rk}(K[P',Q']) = 1$.
  \textbf{Theorem~5.4} (``Glueing the tail''): Conversely,
  $(\partial^p, \widetilde{Q}' + \widetilde{Q}_{-p})$ recovers
  a solution to the string equation.  Together with
  \textbf{Theorem~5.2}, this gives a one-to-one correspondence
  summarized in \textbf{Corollary~5.1}: five equivalent objects
  (normalized solutions, normal string pairs, commuting operators,
  normal pairs, spectral data $(C,p,\mathcal{F},z)$).

\item \textbf{Construction of the contradictory family (Section~6.1).}
  \textbf{Lemma~6.1}: There exists an automorphism
  $\varphi_t \in \mathrm{Aut}_K(A_1)$ depending on a parameter~$t$
  such that $P_n := \varphi_t(P)$ has subrectangular type with the
  \emph{right vertical side} of its Newton polygon consisting of a
  single monomial.  \textbf{Lemma~6.2}: Applying
  $\Phi_{N,1}$ (a specific endomorphism scaling orders by~$N$) to
  $(P_n, Q_n)$ produces operators whose orders grow with~$N$ while
  preserving the sequential GCD structure.

\item \textbf{Explicit contradiction (Section~6.2).}
  The proof culminates in a 7-step calculation.  Put
  $\widetilde{Q}_{N,I}$ for the operators obtained by applying
  Theorem~4.1 to the pair $(\Phi_{N,1}(P_n), \Phi_{N,1}(Q_n))$.
  \begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Steps~1--4}: Establish the body-tail decomposition
    $\widetilde{Q}_{N,i+1} = (\text{body})^{n_i} -
    \varepsilon_i^{n_i}\partial^{\tilde{p}m_i} + \text{tail}$
    (equation~(6.9)) and identify the relevant homogeneous component
    $(\widetilde{Q}_{N,I})_{\mathrm{ord}\,Q_{N,I}-N-1}$.
  \item \textbf{Step~5}: Body-tail decomposition shows this component
    is the first with $\mathrm{Sdeg}_A > 0$.
  \item \textbf{Step~6}: \textbf{Lemma~6.4} calculates the tail
    component, giving a formula involving a
    $\widetilde{d}$-symmetrization~$\tilde{u}$ and a constant~$c_0$
    depending on the orders of the $Q_{N,j}$.
  \item \textbf{Step~7}: Combining the conjugation by the auxiliary
    operator~$\widetilde{S}$ from Lemma~5.1 with the averaging of vector
    components yields the final formula:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:zheglov-contradiction}
    c_{N,I;r(I)} = \varepsilon_I\!\left((-dn+ln)\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}
    - \tfrac{1}{2n(Nd+l)} - \textstyle\sum_{i=0}^{I-1}
    \mathrm{ord}(Q_i) \cdot \tfrac{p-1}{2p}\right)
    + A\tfrac{N^2}{Nd+l} + B\tfrac{N}{Nd+l}
    + C\tfrac{1}{Nd+l} + b\right).
    \end{equation}
    Since $A \neq 0$, this expression depends non-trivially on~$N$,
    contradicting \textbf{Lemma~6.3} (from Lemma~5.1) which asserts
    that the distinguished coefficient~$c_{N,I;r(I)}$ is independent
    of~$N$.
  \end{itemize}
  Conclusion: no DC-pairs exist, so
  $\mathrm{End}(A_1)\!\setminus\!\{0\} = \mathrm{Aut}(A_1)$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Connection to Our Cascade Analysis}

Our cascade analysis and Zheglov's proof are \emph{complementary
approaches} to the same conjecture.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Zheglov (top-down, algebraic):}
$A_1$ endomorphism $\to$ Schur--Sato theory $\to$ normal form $\to$
contradiction with subrectangular constraint $\to$ must be
automorphism.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Our approach (bottom-up, computational):} Keller pair
$(P, Q)$ $\to$ cascade constraints on $P$-coefficients $\to$ finitely
many $n$-independent branches (Proposition~\ref{prop:n-indep}) $\to$
all branches produce automorphisms (verified for $m \leq 4$).

\medskip

The bridge between the two approaches is the Tsuchimoto--Belov-Kanel--Kontsevich
correspondence: a Keller pair $(P, Q)$ with $\deg_y P = m$ and $\det
J(F) = 1$ maps to an $A_1$-endomorphism $\varphi(x) = \widetilde{P},\,
\varphi(\partial) = \widetilde{Q}$ with $[\widetilde{Q},
\widetilde{P}] = 1$, where $\mathrm{ord}(\widetilde{Q}) = m$.

\begin{example}
Our $m = 3$ canonical Keller pair $P = y^3 + dy + x$,
$Q = \alpha x + (1 + d\alpha)y + \alpha y^3$ (with $\det J(F) = 1$
for all $\alpha, d$) maps to the $A_1$-endomorphism
$\varphi(\partial) = \alpha x + (1+d\alpha)\partial + \alpha\partial^3$,
$\varphi(x) = \partial^3 + d\partial + x$,
with $\mathrm{ord}(\varphi(\partial)) = 3$ and $\deg_x(\varphi(x)) = 1$.
Since $\gcd(3, 1) = 1$, Dixmier's original 1968 theorem already
implies this is an automorphism---consistent with our cascade finding.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Where our cascade provides independent evidence]
\label{rem:cascade-zheglov}
For $m = 4$, the non-coprime branch (Section~\ref{sec:auto-px-neq-1})
has $\mathrm{ord}(\varphi(\partial)) = 4$ and $\deg_x(\varphi(x)) = 2$
with $\gcd(4, 2) = 2$. This is precisely the regime where coprimality
fails and Zheglov's proof does its heavy lifting. Our \emph{independent}
verification that both $m = 4$ branches yield automorphisms provides
computational evidence supporting Zheglov's theorem in the first
non-trivial case beyond the coprime regime.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Critical Points for Verification}

Based on a detailed reading of the complete 78-page paper (v5,
January 2026), we identify the following specific points that require
careful peer verification, ordered by potential impact.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item \textbf{$N$-independence of distinguished coefficients
  (Lemma~6.3, from Lemma~5.1).}
  This is the single most critical claim: the distinguished
  coefficient~$c_{N,I;r(I)}$ does not depend on~$N$.  The
  assertion follows from Lemma~5.1's statement that the
  coefficients~$c_i$ ``don't depend on the choice of a sequence~$F_i$
  with the same end of sequential GCD sequence.''  The proof constructs
  auxiliary operators~$\widetilde{S}_k$ as products of elementary
  operators $\widetilde{S}_l = 1 + s_l\widetilde{D}^{-l}$ and
  shows uniqueness via a Vandermonde-type argument on constant
  coefficients.  If this $N$-independence fails, the entire
  contradiction~\eqref{eq:zheglov-contradiction} collapses.

\item \textbf{The $A \neq 0$ claim in the final formula.}
  Equation~\eqref{eq:zheglov-contradiction} produces a contradiction
  only if the coefficient~$A$ of~$N^2/(Nd+l)$ is nonzero.  The
  paper asserts this since $A$ arises from the leading term of the
  body-tail decomposition (equation~(6.9)), but an explicit derivation
  showing $A \neq 0$ under the subrectangular constraints deserves
  independent verification.

\item \textbf{Lemma~5.1: existence and uniqueness of
  $\widetilde{S}$.}
  The construction requires finding a monic invertible operator in
  $K^{\oplus p}((\widetilde{D}^{-1}))$ whose coefficients are
  $\widetilde{D}^{d_i}$-invariant, with additional conditions
  $s_{l,i} = 0$ for $i = 0,\ldots,d_k - 1$ and all~$l$.  The
  proof uses induction on the index of the operator~$S = \widetilde{S}T$,
  with each step invoking an explicit Vandermonde system
  (equation~(5.8)).  The invertibility of the Vandermonde matrix
  (with powers of natural numbers) is standard, but the passage from
  finite to infinite products of elementary operators needs the
  convergence of a Cauchy system in the pseudo-valuation topology.

\item \textbf{Theorem~5.1 and~5.4 (cutting and glueing the tail).}
  The correspondence between string equation solutions and commuting
  operators relies on the Burchnall--Chaundy--Krichever theory
  (Wilson's theorem, Sato's theorem) applied to the ring
  $B' = \hat{\Phi}(K[\partial^p, \widetilde{Q} - \widetilde{Q}_{-p}])$.
  The key claim is that $B' \simeq B$ (the subring generated by the
  original pair), which uses the special form of the tail
  $\widetilde{Q}_{-p}$ preserving the field~$F = K[\widetilde{D}]$.
  The proof that the Sato operator~$S_{cl}$ is also normalized
  (Step~3 of the proof of Theorem~5.2) requires checking that the
  normalisation condition $A_p(0)$ is preserved through the
  correspondence.

\item \textbf{Head-chopping termination (Theorem~4.1).}
  The sequence~$M_k$ must strictly decrease at each step.  The proof
  (Lemma~4.4, items~3--4) distinguishes the cases where the
  polynomial~$g$ (from the Vandermonde formula of Lemma~4.1) has one
  root versus multiple roots.  When $g$ has one root, the order
  bound $\mathrm{ord}(\widetilde{U}_{k+1}) =
  \mathrm{ord}(\widetilde{U}_k)n_k - M_k + 1$ is tight, and the
  ODE~(4.10) from Lemma~4.3 constrains the solution~$H$.  The
  dichotomy $l/d < 1$ versus $l = d$ (footnote~21) needs checking in
  all cases.

\item \textbf{Preservation of subrectangularity (Theorem~4.2).}
  The inductive proof that all operators~$Q_i$ in the head-chopping
  sequence are of subrectangular type is 5~pages of case analysis
  (pages~44--48).  Each case reduces to comparing Newton polygon
  vertices and invoking Lemma~4.4.  The argument splits into: (i)
  $\{f_{1,0}(Q_i), f_{1,0}(P)\} = 0$ versus $\neq 0$, (ii)
  $\tilde{g}$ having one root versus multiple roots, with sub-cases
  for divisibility by~$d_2$.  The complexity of this case analysis
  makes independent verification essential.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{remark}[Paper evolution]
The paper grew from 25~pages (v1, October 2024) to 78~pages (v5,
January 2026) over five revisions.  The most significant addition was
Section~5 (``DC-pairs and the string equation,'' 13~pages) added in
v4, which provides the correspondence theory---the most technically
novel and potentially vulnerable part of the argument.  Sections~2--4
draw on the established Guo--Zheglov normal form
theory~\cite{GuoZheglov1, GuoZheglov2, GuoZheglov3} and the
GGV shape constraints~\cite{GGV}, while Section~6 combines both
strands into the final contradiction.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Related approaches]
Han--Pan--Chen~\cite{HanPanChen} take a complementary approach using
Joseph normal forms for elements of~$A_1$: any nilpotent or semisimple
element~$z$ has a \emph{Joseph normal form} corresponding to a unique pair
$(k, n)$ with $k \geq n \geq 0$. They reformulate DC$_1$ as: for any nilpotent
$z$ with Joseph pair $(k, n)$ and $k > n \geq 1$, there is no $w \in A_1$
with $[z, w] = 1$.  They prove this when $k$ or $n$ is prime (extending
the coprime case).
\end{remark}

\section{Concrete Research Questions}
%% ====================================================================

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{PRIORITY: Leave the one-point-at-infinity world.}
  By Section~\ref{sec:scope}, every pair treated by the cascade
  framework ($P_m = y^m$) is already known to be an automorphism
  (Abhyankar), and a minimal counterexample has
  $P_m = a\,h^{m/d}$, $Q_n = b\,h^{n/d}$ with $h$ having at least
  two distinct linear factors, $m \nmid n$, $n \nmid m$,
  $d = \gcd(m,n) \geq 16$. The cascade must be
  re-derived in this setting:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Top-level operator}: with $P_m = h^{a}$ the leading
    cascade operator is $J(h^a, \cdot) = a\,h^{a-1} J(h, \cdot)$,
    whose kernel on forms of degree~$k$ is $\kk\,h^{k/d}$
    (nonzero only for $d \mid k$) and whose image is divisible
    by~$h^{a-1}$. Work out the first few cascade levels symbolically
    for small~$d$ and compare with the Newton-polygon constraints of
    Heitmann~\cite{Heitmann1990} and
    Guccione--Guccione--Valqui~\cite{GGV,GGV2014}.
  \item \emph{Rank-jump loci}: in the divisible case, every Keller pair
    with $P_x \neq 1$ sits where the cascade's linear system drops rank
    (Proposition~\ref{prop:cascade-identity-demystified}). Characterize
    the rank-jump locus for $m = 4, 5$ and test whether it is always
    contained in ``$P$ is a polynomial in a lower-degree coordinate~$g$
    and~$y$'' (as in Proposition~\ref{prop:px-neq-1-auto}(b)).
  \end{itemize}
  The identity $\det J(P, Q_{\mathrm{cas}}) = P_x$
  (Conjecture~\ref{conj:cascade-identity}) is equivalent to
  $\mathcal{K}(P) = \operatorname{span}(P^2,\ldots,P^{r-1})$ and is
  therefore a statement about generic~$P$ only; it is no longer a
  priority.

\item \textbf{Verify Zheglov's DC$_1$ proof.}
  A detailed analysis of the proof structure appears in
  Section~\ref{sec:zheglov-analysis}.  The six critical verification
  points are listed there.
  Our cascade analysis (Remark~\ref{rem:cascade-zheglov}) provides
  \emph{independent} computational evidence for the first non-trivial
  case beyond the coprime regime ($m = 4$, $\gcd = 2$).

\item \textbf{Prove all cascade branches produce automorphisms.}
  For each~$m$, the $D < m$ cascade constraints have finitely many
  solution branches ($n$-independent by
  Proposition~\ref{prop:n-indep}). For $m = 3$: one branch ($P_x = 1$),
  confirmed automorphism. For $m = 4$: two branches, both confirmed
  automorphisms (Propositions~\ref{prop:spurious}
  and~\ref{prop:px-neq-1-auto}). This branch-by-branch approach
  is an independent route to JC$_2$.

\item \textbf{Extend computational verification:}
  \begin{itemize}
  \item Verify the Cascade Identity (full back-substitution
    + symbolic Jacobian) for $m = 6, 7$.
  \item Test the universal excess pattern for $m = 8, 9$.
  \item Enumerate all cascade branches for $m = 5$
    (Gr\"obner basis of the $D < m$ constraints).
  \end{itemize}

\item \textbf{Connection to dimension $\geq 3$:} Analyze how the
  counterexamples of Alpoge~\cite{Alpoge2026} and
  Gallagher~\cite{Gallagher2026} relate to the cascade structure.
  In dimension~$2$, every branch of the cascade constraints yields
  an automorphism; in dimension~$\geq 3$, the analogous
  structure may break down.
\end{enumerate}
